# Model Answers — Writing (Paper 1)

Four more annotated model compositions, three situational writing models, and a marking rubric so a parent can grade a composition at home without guessing.

---

## Part 1 — How to Mark a Composition at Home

You do not need to be an English teacher. You need to apply the same two scales the examiner uses, and be consistent.

### Content — 20 marks

| Band | Marks | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | 17–20 | Clearly on theme. One well-chosen incident, developed in depth. The climax is expanded, not rushed. We understand what the character thought and felt, and the ending shows genuine change or insight. |
| **B** | 13–16 | On theme, complete plot, sensible detail. The climax is present but a little thin. Some insight, though the ending may state the lesson rather than show it. |
| **C** | 9–12 | Relevant to the theme but thin, or too many events crammed in. Detail is general. Little sense of what the character felt. |
| **D** | 4–8 | Only loosely connected to the theme, or the plot is confused or incomplete. |
| **E** | 0–3 | Off theme, or too short to judge. |

### Language — 20 marks

| Band | Marks | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | 17–20 | Almost no grammatical errors. Varied sentence structures and openings. Vocabulary is precise and used correctly. Paragraphing is logical. Dialogue is punctuated correctly. |
| **B** | 13–16 | A few errors that do not obscure meaning. Some sentence variety. Vocabulary is adequate with occasional reaching. |
| **C** | 9–12 | Frequent errors, mostly in tense and subject–verb agreement. Mostly simple sentences. Repetitive vocabulary (*said, went, nice, very*). |
| **D** | 4–8 | Errors in most sentences; meaning sometimes unclear. |
| **E** | 0–3 | Very difficult to follow. |

### The three questions to ask before assigning a mark

1. **Could I state the theme after reading it, without seeing the question?** If not, content cannot reach band A.
2. **How many words does the climax take?** If under 60, it has been rushed — cap content at band B.
3. **Count the tense and agreement errors.** Zero to two → band A language. Three to six → band B. Seven or more → band C or below.

> **Give one specific instruction, not five.** A child who is told "improve your vocabulary, add more description, watch your tenses, vary your sentences and write a better ending" will change nothing. A child told "this week, expand the climax to at least eight sentences" will do it.

## Part 2 — Model Compositions

### Model 3 · Theme: Perseverance

> **The Third Attempt**
>
> I had failed the swimming test twice, and the second time was worse than the first.
>
> Everyone in my class had earned their bronze badge by October. Mine was still a blank space on the noticeboard where my name should have been, and every Tuesday Miss Faridah would read out the list of pupils going to the deep-end lane, and every Tuesday my name was not on it.
>
> The problem was not my arms. It was my breathing. Somewhere around the twelfth metre, my chest would tighten and my head would come up too early, and after that the whole stroke fell apart. I had been told what to do a hundred times. Knowing was not the difficulty.
>
> The third test was in November. I asked my father to take me to the public pool on Saturday mornings, and for six weeks we went at seven, before the crowds. He could not swim himself. He sat on the plastic bench at the shallow end with a flask of coffee and counted my strokes out loud, and when I stopped halfway he did not say anything encouraging at all. He just said the number I had reached, and waited.
>
> Fourteen. Then nineteen. Then twenty-two, then twenty-two again for three weeks in a row.
>
> On the last Saturday before the test, I reached thirty-one and touched the far wall. I stood up in the shallow end with water streaming off my face and looked at my father. He capped his flask and said, "Right. Breakfast."
>
> I passed on the Tuesday, in the second-slowest time in the class.
>
> The badge is in a drawer somewhere now and I could not tell you where. But I remember every one of those Saturday mornings, and I remember that twenty-two is not a wall. It is just a number you have not passed yet.

**Why it scores well:** one narrow problem (breathing at twelve metres) rather than a vague "I was bad at swimming"; the father's restraint is more moving than encouragement would be; the numbers do the emotional work; the ending refuses the easy triumph — she finishes second-slowest — and lands on a genuine, earned idea.

### Model 4 · Theme: Kindness

> **The Blue Chair**
>
> Mr Tan had sat on the same blue plastic chair outside the provision shop for as long as I had been alive.
>
> He was there every morning when I left for school and every evening when I came back, one hand resting on a walking stick he did not really need, watching the carpark the way other people watch television. He was not friendly. If you greeted him he would nod, once, and look away again. My friends called him the guard.
>
> In March the chair was empty for four days.
>
> I noticed on the second day and said nothing. On the fourth day my grandmother asked the shopkeeper, who said Mr Tan's sister in Ipoh had passed away and he had gone up for the funeral, and that he had no one to feed his birds.
>
> I did not know he kept birds.
>
> That evening my grandmother handed me a key and a bag of millet and told me the flat number. I climbed to the eleventh floor expecting one cage. There were four, hanging along the corridor outside his door, and the moment I appeared all of them began to sing at once — a wall of sound so sudden and so loud that I nearly dropped the bag.
>
> I fed them every day for nine days.
>
> When Mr Tan came back, he did not thank me. He nodded once, the way he always did, and looked away. I was, I admit, a little disappointed.
>
> Then on Monday morning there was a second blue chair beside his, empty, and it was still there on Tuesday, and on Wednesday, and it is there now.
>
> I sit in it sometimes, before school. We do not talk much. It turns out we do not have to.

**Why it scores well:** the kindness is small and practical, not heroic; the birds are a specific, surprising detail; the reward is withheld and then delivered as an object rather than a speech; the final three sentences show the change instead of announcing it.

### Model 5 · Theme: A Misunderstanding

> **What I Thought I Saw**
>
> I saw Priya take the money. I was completely certain, which is the dangerous part.
>
> Our class had been collecting for the Children's Home for three weeks, and the tin sat on the shelf beside the door, growing heavier. On Thursday, during recess, I came back for my water bottle and saw Priya standing at the shelf with the tin in both hands and her back to the door.
>
> She turned. She saw me. And her face did something complicated that I read, instantly and confidently, as guilt.
>
> I told Wei Jie at lunch. I made him promise not to tell anyone, which is a promise nobody has ever kept in the history of our school. By Friday morning, four people had stopped sitting with her. By Friday afternoon, six had.
>
> Priya did not defend herself. She did not say anything at all, which I took as further proof.
>
> On Monday, Mrs Kaur asked the class to give her their full attention and announced that the collection had reached four hundred and twelve dollars — sixty of which had been added by one pupil who had asked to remain anonymous, from money she had saved for a bicycle.
>
> She did not say a name. She did not have to. I watched Priya study the surface of her desk with tremendous concentration, and I felt the floor tilt underneath me.
>
> I apologised that afternoon. She said it was fine. It was not fine, and we both knew it, and it took most of the following term before she sat next to me again by choice.
>
> I still see that moment at the shelf sometimes — her turning, her face. I know now that what I read as guilt was simply the look of somebody caught doing something private. I have never trusted my own certainty in quite the same way since.

**Why it scores well:** the reader is misled alongside the narrator; the consequences escalate believably through gossip; the reveal is indirect — the teacher never names Priya; the apology is not accepted neatly, which is far more honest than instant forgiveness.

### Model 6 · Theme: Facing a Fear

> **Two Metres**
>
> The diving board at the sports complex is two metres high. I have measured it. Two metres is nothing. You could stack two of my father on top of each other and still have room.
>
> I had climbed the ladder four times that year and come back down four times, past whoever was waiting behind me, with my ears burning.
>
> The fifth time was the day of the swimming carnival, and there was a queue.
>
> I remember the metal rungs, cold and slightly gritty under my feet, and the way the board felt different from the ground — softer, as though it might not hold. I walked to the end. Below me the water had stopped looking like water and started looking like a floor.
>
> Behind me somebody said, "Are you going or not?"
>
> That, strangely, was the thing that helped. Not encouragement. Impatience. I thought: I am going to be standing here for the rest of my life. There is no version of this where I climb down again and feel all right about it.
>
> So I stepped off.
>
> It is not a brave story. I did not dive; I fell, badly, and my legs came apart in the air and the water hit the side of my thigh so hard that the mark was still there a week later. Somebody laughed. I came up coughing and my goggles were somewhere near the bottom of the pool.
>
> But I had gone.
>
> I went again eleven minutes later, and the second time was easier than the first by an amount I cannot describe to anyone who has not stood on that board. The fear had not disappeared. It had simply become something I had already survived once, which turns out to be a completely different thing.

**Why it scores well:** the fear is small and specific — two metres, five attempts — which makes it credible; the turning point is deliberately unromantic; the dive itself goes badly, which raises the honesty; the last sentence names the insight precisely without preaching.

## Part 3 — Situational Writing Models

### Model A · Formal complaint

> **Task:** You bought a school bag from Bright Stationers two weeks ago. The zip broke after three days. The shop has not replied to your earlier message. Write to the manager. Include: what you bought and when, what is wrong, what you have already done, and what you would like the shop to do.

```
To: manager@brightstationers.com.sg
Subject: Faulty school bag purchased on 2 August (receipt no. 40318)

Dear Sir or Madam,

I am writing about a school bag I purchased from your Sengkang branch on
2 August. The receipt number is 40318.

The bag was in good condition when I bought it, but the main zip came away
from the fabric after three days of ordinary use. I have not dropped the bag
or overloaded it, and the damage appears to be a fault in the stitching rather
than the result of misuse.

I sent a message through the contact form on your website on 8 August but have
not received a reply. As the bag is still within the one-month warranty printed
on the receipt, I would be grateful if you could either repair the zip or
exchange the bag for one of the same model.

I have kept the receipt and the original packaging, and I can bring the bag to
the store at any time that is convenient. I look forward to hearing from you.

Yours faithfully,
Rachel Tan
```

**Marker's view:** all four bullet points covered, each with one supporting detail. Consistently formal — no contractions, no exclamation marks. The request is specific ("repair or exchange") rather than vague ("do something about it"). It offers cooperation at the end, which reads as reasonable rather than aggressive.

### Model B · Report to the principal

> **Task:** You are the Primary 6 class chairperson. Write a report to your principal on the class's visit to a nursing home. Include: what the class did, how the residents responded, one problem faced, and one recommendation.

```
Report on the Primary 6 Resilience Visit to Sunrise Nursing Home
By: Rachel Tan, Class Chairperson
Date: 14 August 2026

Purpose
This report describes our class's visit to Sunrise Nursing Home on 8 August and
offers a recommendation for future visits.

What we did
Thirty-two pupils visited the home from 9 a.m. to 12 noon. We were divided into
four groups. Two groups performed songs and a short skit in the common hall,
while the other two spent the morning talking with residents in the day room
and helping with a simple craft activity.

Response of the residents
The response was warmer than we had expected. Several residents joined in the
singing, and one gentleman taught a group of pupils a Hokkien song none of us
had heard before. Staff told us afterwards that the day room is usually quiet
on weekday mornings.

Problem faced
The main difficulty was language. Many residents speak mainly dialect, and most
of our pupils could not hold a conversation beyond a greeting. Some pupils
became discouraged and stopped trying after the first ten minutes.

Recommendation
I recommend that pupils learn ten to fifteen useful phrases in Hokkien, Teochew
and Cantonese before the next visit, and that each group includes at least one
pupil who speaks a dialect at home. This would help conversations begin more
easily and would prevent pupils from giving up early.
```

**Marker's view:** headings, factual tone, no chatty language. Each bullet point gets its own section. The problem is stated honestly rather than smoothed over, and the recommendation follows directly from it — that link is where the marks are.

### Model C · Informal thank-you

> **Task:** Your neighbour looked after your rabbit for two weeks while your family was overseas. Write to thank her. Include: what she did, something she did especially well, how the rabbit is now, and an offer to return the favour.

```
To: mrs.rahman@email.com
Subject: Thank you for looking after Momo!

Dear Mrs Rahman,

Thank you so much for taking care of Momo while we were away in Penang. Two
weeks is a long time to look after somebody else's rabbit, and we are all very
grateful.

What I appreciated most was that you kept to his feeding times exactly. Momo is
fussy about routine, and last year when he stayed at the pet shop he stopped
eating for two days. This time he was completely settled when we got back — and
I noticed you had even been giving him the coriander he loves.

He is doing very well. He has gone straight back to sleeping under the sofa and
chewing the corner of my school bag, so nothing has changed at all.

If you and Mr Rahman are ever away, we would be very happy to water your plants
or feed your cat. Please just let us know, even at short notice.

Thank you again.

Your neighbour,
Rachel
```

**Marker's view:** warm but not gushing. The specific detail — the coriander, the pet shop last year — is what makes the thanks feel real rather than formulaic. The offer at the end is concrete, and the tone stays informal from greeting to sign-off.
