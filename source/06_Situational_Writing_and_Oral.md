# PART A — SITUATIONAL WRITING (15 marks, ~20 minutes)

## 1. What You Are Marked On

| Component | Marks | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Task Fulfilment** | 9 | Did you cover **every bullet point** in the question, with enough detail? |
| **Language and Organisation** | 6 | Correct tone for the audience, accurate grammar, clear paragraphs, correct format |

> **Task fulfilment is worth more than language here.** A beautifully written response that misses one bullet point loses more marks than a plain response that covers everything. Tick off the bullet points as you write.

---

## 2. PAC — Do This Before Writing (2 minutes)

Every situational writing question contains three clues. Underline them on the question paper.

| | Question to ask | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **P — Purpose** | Why am I writing? (to inform / invite / request / thank / apologise / complain / suggest / report) | Determines your opening line |
| **A — Audience** | Who am I writing to? (friend / teacher / principal / stranger / shop manager) | Determines **formal or informal** tone |
| **C — Context** | What is the situation? What details am I given? | Prevents you from inventing wrong facts |

### The tone test

| Audience | Tone | Greeting | Sign-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Close friend, cousin, sibling | **Informal** | Hi Aisha, / Dear Aisha, | Love, / Your friend, / Bye for now, |
| Teacher you know well | **Semi-formal** | Dear Mrs Tan, | Yours sincerely, |
| Principal, a company, a stranger | **Formal** | Dear Mr Lim, / Dear Sir or Madam, | Yours sincerely, (named) / Yours faithfully, (unnamed) |

**Informal features allowed:** contractions (I'm, don't), exclamation marks, chatty phrases (*Guess what!*, *Can't wait!*), questions to the reader.
**Formal features required:** no contractions, no slang, no exclamation marks, complete sentences, polite requests (*I would be grateful if…*, *Could you kindly…*).

> ⚠️ **Never mix tones.** *"Dear Sir or Madam, Hey, I'm writing about…"* loses language marks immediately.

## 3. Formats

### 3.1 Informal Email

```
To: aisha@email.com
Subject: Our trip to the Science Centre this Saturday

Hi Aisha,

[Opening: greeting + reason for writing]

[Body paragraph(s): cover every bullet point]

[Closing: what you want them to do, or a warm sign-off line]

Your friend,
Rachel
```

### 3.2 Formal Email or Letter

```
To: enquiries@library.gov.sg
Subject: Enquiry about the Junior Reading Programme

Dear Sir or Madam,

[Opening: state who you are and why you are writing, in one sentence]

[Body: cover the bullet points, one idea per paragraph]

[Closing: state what response you would like, and thank them]

Yours faithfully,
Rachel Tan
Primary 6 Resilience
Sengkang Primary School
```

> **Yours sincerely** when you know the person's name (*Dear Mrs Tan*).
> **Yours faithfully** when you do not (*Dear Sir or Madam*).

### 3.3 Report (to a teacher or committee)

```
Report on the Primary 6 Recycling Drive
By: Rachel Tan, Primary 6 Resilience
Date: 14 August 2026

Purpose
[One or two lines on why the report was written]

Details of the event
[What happened, when, where, who was involved]

Feedback / Findings
[What went well, what did not]

Recommendations
[What should be done differently next time]
```

Reports use **headings**, **factual language**, and **no personal chatter**.

### 3.4 Short Message or Note

Keep it brief and cover all points. Still start with a greeting and end with your name.

## 4. Worked Example

> **Question:** Your school library is starting a Book Buddy Programme. You would like your friend Daniel, who has moved to another school, to know about it and to join a joint online session. Write an email to Daniel.
>
> You should include:
> - what the programme is about
> - when and where it takes place
> - why you think he would enjoy it
> - how he can join the online session

### Planning (1 minute)
- **P:** to inform + invite
- **A:** a friend → **informal**
- **C:** four bullet points, all must be covered

### Model answer

```
To: daniel.ong@email.com
Subject: You have to join our Book Buddy Programme!

Hi Daniel,

How have you been settling in at your new school? I have some news that I think
you will love.

Our library has just started a Book Buddy Programme. Each of us is paired with a
reading partner, and every week we read the same book and then discuss our
favourite parts. There are also small challenges, such as designing a new cover
for the book we have read.

The sessions are held every Wednesday from 2 p.m. to 3 p.m. in the school
library. Since you are no longer here, the good news is that there is an online
session on the last Wednesday of each month, and pupils from other schools are
welcome to join.

I really think you would enjoy this. You were always the one recommending
mystery novels to me, and this month's book is a detective story set in
Singapore. It would be just like our old recess conversations.

To join the online session, simply fill in the form on the school library
website and pick "Guest Reader" when it asks for your role. Let me know once
you have signed up so that I can request to be your buddy.

Hope to see you online soon!

Your friend,
Rachel
```

**Why this scores well:** all four bullet points covered with a detail each · consistent informal tone · logical paragraphing · a clear call to action at the end.

## 5. Situational Writing Checklist

- [ ] Did I identify **Purpose, Audience and Context**?
- [ ] Have I covered **every bullet point**? (Tick them off on the question paper.)
- [ ] Did I add **one supporting detail** to each point, not just repeat the bullet?
- [ ] Is the **tone** consistent from greeting to sign-off?
- [ ] Correct **format** (To / Subject / greeting / paragraphs / sign-off / name)?
- [ ] Did I invent any information that **contradicts** the question?
- [ ] Grammar, spelling, punctuation checked?

## 6. Practice Prompts

1. Write to your form teacher explaining why you were absent from the class outing, apologising, and asking what work you missed.
2. Write to the manager of a bookshop about a damaged book you bought, describing the problem, when you bought it, and what you would like done.
3. Write to your cousin overseas inviting her to your school's Racial Harmony Day performance, including what you will perform, when it is, and how she can watch it online.
4. Write a report to your principal on the Primary 5 camp, covering the activities, what pupils enjoyed, one problem faced, and one recommendation.
5. Write to your neighbour thanking her for looking after your rabbit while your family was away, mentioning something she did well and offering to return the favour.
6. Write to the community centre asking about their holiday coding workshop: the dates, the fee, whether beginners are welcome, and how to register.

# PART B — ORAL COMMUNICATION (Paper 4, 30 marks)

## 1. Reading Aloud (10 marks)

You are given a short passage and about **five minutes** to prepare.

### What examiners listen for
| | |
|---|---|
| **Pronunciation** | Clear consonants, especially final sounds (-s, -ed, -t, -k) |
| **Fluency** | Smooth, no stumbling, no long pauses in the middle of a phrase |
| **Expression** | Voice matches the meaning — rising for questions, warmth for happy content |
| **Pace** | Not too fast. Slower than you think is right. |

### Preparation routine (use every second of the five minutes)
1. **Read the whole passage silently once** to understand what it is about. You cannot read expressively if you do not know the meaning.
2. **Mark your pauses** with a pencil slash: `/` at commas, `//` at full stops.
3. **Circle difficult words** and say them quietly to yourself twice.
4. **Underline words to stress** — usually the key noun or verb in each sentence.
5. **Note any question marks and exclamation marks** — your voice must change there.

### Common Singapore-specific pronunciation points to watch
- **Final consonants**: say "wan**t**ed", "as**ked**", "bo**th**", not "wan", "ask", "bof"
- **-s endings**: "the boy**s** run", "he walk**s**"
- **th sounds**: *three* (not *tree*), *this* (not *dis*)
- **v vs w**: *very* (not *wery*)
- **Word stress**: com-FOR-ta-ble, in-TER-es-ting, VEG-e-ta-ble

### During the reading
- Look up briefly at the examiner once or twice — but do not lose your place
- If you make a mistake, **repeat only that word** and carry on calmly. Do not restart the sentence.
- Breathe at the full stops. Rushing is the most common cause of lost marks.

## 2. Stimulus-Based Conversation (20 marks)

You are shown a **video clip**, then asked **three questions** by the examiner.

### The three questions usually follow this pattern

| Question | Type | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Q1** | About the video | Give your view on what you saw, and explain why |
| **Q2** | About your personal experience | Tell a short, specific story from your own life |
| **Q3** | A broader opinion question | Give an opinion, reasons, and a suggestion |

### The golden rule: **never give a one-sentence answer**

Aim for **4–6 sentences** per answer. Use the **PEEL** structure:

| | |
|---|---|
| **P — Point** | State your answer clearly |
| **E — Elaborate** | Explain what you mean |
| **E — Example** | Give a personal example or a specific instance |
| **L — Link** | Round off with a conclusion or a suggestion |

### Worked example

> **Examiner:** *Do you think students should be encouraged to take part in community projects?*

> **Weak answer:** "Yes, because it is good."
>
> **Strong answer (PEEL):**
> **P** — "Yes, I strongly believe students should take part in community projects.
> **E** — This is because such projects teach us responsibility and help us understand people whose lives are different from our own, which we cannot learn from textbooks alone.
> **E** — Last year, my class helped to pack and deliver food parcels to elderly residents in our neighbourhood. I spoke to one gentleman who lived alone, and I realised how much a short conversation meant to him.
> **L** — Because of that experience, I think every school should organise at least one community project a year, so that more students get the chance to see this for themselves."

### Useful phrases

**Giving an opinion:** In my opinion… · I strongly believe that… · From my point of view… · I feel that…
**Explaining:** This is because… · The reason for this is… · What I mean by this is…
**Giving examples:** For instance… · Let me give you an example… · This reminds me of a time when…
**Agreeing partly:** I agree to a certain extent, however… · While that is true, I also think…
**Concluding:** All things considered… · That is why I believe… · Overall…

**Buying thinking time (better than "erm"):** *That is an interesting question…* · *Let me think about that for a moment…*

### Preparation that actually works
- **Talk about the news at dinner.** Pick one everyday topic a week — recycling, screen time, road safety, kindness to seniors, food waste, exercise, saving money — and discuss it for five minutes.
- **Build a personal-story bank.** Have 5–6 real experiences ready that can be adapted to many questions: a time you helped someone, a time you failed, a school event, a family trip, a time you learnt something, a time you were disappointed. Most oral questions can be answered using one of these.
- **Practise out loud, not in your head.** Record yourself on a phone and listen back once.

### Delivery
- **Eye contact** with the examiner
- **Sit up straight**, hands still
- **Speak at a moderate pace** and finish your sentences
- **Smile** — examiners mark on engagement as well as content

## 3. Listening Comprehension (Paper 3, 20 marks) — Quick Tips

- The passage is read **twice**. Use the **first reading to understand** and the **second to confirm**.
- Read the questions and all the options **before** the recording starts.
- Underline key words in each question so you know what to listen for.
- Distractors are common: the recording often mentions all four options — listen for which one actually answers the question.
- Do not leave any question blank. Make your best choice and move on.
