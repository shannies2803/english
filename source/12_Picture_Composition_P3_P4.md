# Picture Composition — Primary 3 and 4

At Primary 3 and 4, composition is a **four-picture series**. The pictures tell a story in order, and the child writes it out in about 120 to 180 words. It is marked out of 20: **10 for content, 10 for language**.

This is a different exam from the Primary 5–6 one. There is no theme to interpret and no choice of pictures — all four must be used, in order. The skill being tested is telling a clear story in correct past tense, not inventing a plot.

---

## What the marks are for

| Content — 10 | | Language — 10 | |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9–10 · All four pictures used in order, with extra details of your own and a clear ending | 10 | 9–10 · Past tense throughout, full sentences, capital letters and full stops correct, some good words | 10 |
| 7–8 · All four pictures used, but few details added | 8 | 7–8 · A few mistakes in tense or punctuation, but easy to read | 8 |
| 5–6 · One picture missed, or the order is muddled | 6 | 5–6 · Several tense or full-stop mistakes; short repeated sentences | 6 |
| 3–4 · Only two pictures used, or the story is hard to follow | 4 | 3–4 · Many mistakes; meaning sometimes unclear | 4 |
| 0–2 · Does not match the pictures | 2 | 0–2 · Very hard to read | 2 |

### The three things that lift a P3 composition

1. **Add what the pictures cannot show.** The day, the weather, a name, why the child was there, what they were thinking. A story that only describes the four drawings stays in the middle band.
2. **Stay in the past tense.** This is where most language marks are lost at this level. *I saw, I ran, she smiled* — not *I see, I run, she smiles*.
3. **End with a feeling and a reason.** "I was happy" earns little. "I was happy, but not because of the chocolates" earns a great deal more.

### Before writing — three boxes, two minutes

```
1 BEGINNING — who, where, when
2 PROBLEM — what went wrong (pictures 2 and 3)
3 ENDING — how it was solved, and how you felt
```

## The twelve tasks

*Write your own story first. Then read the model and the note underneath it.*

### 1. The Wallet on the Bench  ·  *Honesty*

**The four pictures**

1. A boy sees a wallet on a bench
2. He looks around but nobody is there
3. He hands it to a shopkeeper
4. A woman thanks him

**Model composition**

> Last Saturday afternoon, I was walking home through the park when I saw a brown wallet lying on a wooden bench.
>
> I picked it up. It was heavy, and when I opened it I could see several notes inside. I looked all around the park, but there was nobody nearby. My heart began to beat faster. For a moment I thought about the model aeroplane I had been saving for.
>
> Then I remembered what my mother always says: never take what is not yours. I walked quickly to the provision shop at the corner and gave the wallet to Uncle Lim, the shopkeeper. He wrote down my name and my block number.
>
> Two days later, a lady came to our flat. She was the owner of the wallet and she had been looking for it everywhere. She thanked me warmly and gave me a small box of chocolates.
>
> I was very happy, but not because of the chocolates. I was happy because I had done the right thing.

**What makes it work:** All four pictures are used in order. The writer adds details the pictures do not show — the day, the park, the model aeroplane, Uncle Lim's name — which is what lifts content into the top band. The ending says how he felt and why, instead of stopping at 'she thanked me'.

### 2. The Fallen Groceries  ·  *Kindness*

**The four pictures**

1. An elderly man carries a heavy bag
2. The bag tears and the fruit rolls away
3. A girl helps him pick everything up
4. She carries the bag to his door

**Model composition**

> One evening, I was walking back from the playground when I noticed an elderly man struggling with a large paper bag of groceries. He was walking very slowly and the bag looked far too heavy for him.
>
> Suddenly, the bottom of the bag tore open. Apples, oranges and a cabbage rolled all over the pavement. The old man bent down stiffly and tried to gather them, but he could hardly reach.
>
> I ran over at once and began picking up the fruit. "Thank you, girl," he said softly. Together we collected everything and put it into my empty school bag.
>
> I offered to carry the bag home for him. He lived on the second floor of the next block, so it did not take long. At his door, he smiled and said that I reminded him of his own granddaughter.
>
> It had taken me only ten minutes, but he looked so pleased. Now I always slow down when I pass an elderly neighbour.

**What makes it work:** Uses all four pictures and adds the school bag, the second floor and the granddaughter — details invented by the writer. The last two sentences show what changed, which is what turns a description of pictures into a story.

### 3. The Kite in the Tree  ·  *Problem solving*

**The four pictures**

1. Two children fly a kite in the field
2. The kite gets stuck high in a tree
3. They ask a gardener for help
4. The kite is flying again

**Model composition**

> Last Sunday morning, my cousin Daniel and I took our new kite to the open field behind our block. The wind was strong and the kite climbed higher and higher until it was only a red dot in the sky.
>
> Then the wind changed direction suddenly. Before I could pull the string, the kite swooped sideways and became tangled in the branches of a tall angsana tree. Daniel and I jumped and jumped, but the kite was far beyond our reach.
>
> We were about to give up when we saw a gardener trimming the hedges nearby. We ran over and explained what had happened. He smiled and said he had exactly the right tool.
>
> Using his long pole, the gardener gently hooked the kite and lowered it down. Amazingly, it was not torn at all.
>
> We thanked him again and again. Within minutes our kite was soaring above the field once more. I have learnt that it is never foolish to ask an adult for help.

**What makes it work:** Good use of specific words — angsana, swooped, tangled, soaring. The problem is solved by asking for help rather than by magic, which keeps the story believable. Note how paragraph 3 builds a small moment of hopelessness before the solution arrives.

### 4. Caught in the Rain  ·  *Friendship*

**The four pictures**

1. A boy walks to school under a grey sky
2. Heavy rain begins and he has no umbrella
3. A friend shares her umbrella
4. They arrive at school dry and laughing

**Model composition**

> One Monday morning, I set off for school without checking the sky. Thick grey clouds were gathering above the void deck, but I was already late and did not want to run back upstairs for my umbrella.
>
> I had walked only halfway when the rain came down. It was not a light drizzle but a proper downpour. Within seconds my shirt was soaked and my school bag was dripping. I stood under a tree, shivering and wondering what to do.
>
> Just then I heard someone calling my name. It was Mei Ling, my classmate, holding a big red umbrella. She hurried over and held it above both of us.
>
> We walked the rest of the way together, our shoulders bumping under the umbrella, laughing every time a car splashed past. By the time we reached the school gate, I was almost dry again.
>
> I thanked Mei Ling and promised to bring an extra umbrella from then on. A good friend, I realised, always appears at the right moment.

**What makes it work:** The first paragraph explains why he has no umbrella, which the pictures cannot show. 'Our shoulders bumping under the umbrella' is a small invented detail that makes the scene real. Notice the past tense is used throughout, without slipping.

### 5. The Spilt Paint  ·  *Owning up*

**The four pictures**

1. Children painting in the art room
2. A boy knocks over a pot of paint
3. He tells the teacher what happened
4. They clean it up together

**Model composition**

> Last Friday, our class had an art lesson in the art room. We were painting posters for Racial Harmony Day, and every table was covered with brushes, water pots and jars of paint.
>
> I reached across the table for a brush without looking. My elbow caught the jar of red paint and knocked it right over. The paint poured across the table and dripped onto the floor in a bright red puddle. For a moment I froze. Nobody had seen me do it.
>
> I thought about saying nothing. Then I put down my brush and walked to Mrs Devi's desk. I told her that I had knocked over the paint and that I was sorry.
>
> Mrs Devi did not scold me at all. She thanked me for telling her straight away, fetched a bucket and some cloths, and helped me clean up the mess.
>
> It took us fifteen minutes. My shoes still have a faint pink mark on them, and I do not mind at all.

**What makes it work:** The short sentence 'For a moment I froze' slows the story at exactly the right point. 'Nobody had seen me do it' shows the temptation, which is what makes owning up worth writing about. The last sentence is understated rather than preachy.

### 6. The Injured Bird  ·  *Caring for animals*

**The four pictures**

1. A girl finds a small bird on the ground
2. She puts it gently into a box
3. Her father takes it to the vet
4. The bird flies away

**Model composition**

> One morning, while I was watering the plants along our corridor, I heard a weak chirping sound. On the ground near the staircase lay a small brown bird. One of its wings was bent at an odd angle and it could not fly.
>
> I ran inside and found an empty shoebox. My mother helped me line it with an old towel so that the bird would be warm. Very carefully, I lifted the tiny creature with both hands and placed it inside. It was lighter than I had expected and its heart was beating very fast.
>
> That afternoon, my father drove us to an animal clinic near the market. The vet examined the bird and told us that the wing was only bruised, not broken. She gave us some medicine and told us to keep the bird quiet for a week.
>
> On the eighth day, we carried the box down to the field and opened it. The bird hopped once, then rose into the air and disappeared over the rooftops.
>
> I felt a little sad, but mostly proud.

**What makes it work:** 'Lighter than I had expected and its heart was beating very fast' is exactly the kind of detail that earns top language marks — it shows the writer imagining the moment rather than describing a drawing. The two-word emotion at the end is honest and avoids a soppy ending.

### 7. The Skipping Rope  ·  *Perseverance*

**The four pictures**

1. A girl trips during the skipping trial while her classmates watch
2. She practises alone in the corridor every evening
3. She misses again and sits down, discouraged
4. On Sports Day she skips without stopping and is given a certificate

**Model composition**

> Last term, our teacher held a skipping trial to choose ten pupils for the Sports Day display. I wanted a place badly, but when my turn came my foot caught the rope on the fourth jump and I stumbled in front of the whole class. Two boys laughed.
>
> That evening, I took my rope down to the corridor outside our flat and practised until it was dark. I did the same the next evening, and the next. Sometimes I managed forty jumps. Sometimes I tripped on the second one and sat down on the cold floor, wanting to give up.
>
> I kept going because my grandmother said that a rope only obeys people who are stubborn.
>
> On Sports Day, I skipped for two whole minutes without a single mistake. Mrs Kaur handed me a certificate and told me she had watched me practising from her window every evening. I was proud, but not of the certificate. I was proud of all the dark evenings nobody saw.

**What makes it work:** All four pictures are used in order, and the writer adds what the drawings cannot show: the term, the ten places, the two boys laughing, the grandmother's line about the rope. Past tense is held throughout. The ending does what a top-band P3 ending does — it names a feeling and then gives an unexpected reason for it, instead of stopping at "I was very happy".

### 8. The Lost Little Boy  ·  *Helping others*

**The four pictures**

1. At the market, a small boy stands alone and cries
2. A girl kneels down and asks him what has happened
3. She takes him to the information counter and an announcement is made
4. His mother runs over and hugs him

**Model composition**

> One Sunday morning, I was helping my father choose fish at the wet market when I heard a child crying somewhere behind the vegetable stalls. I went to look. A little boy of about four was standing alone between two crates, sobbing so hard that he could not speak.
>
> I knelt down so that I was not taller than him and asked him his name. He said it was Ryan, and that he had let go of his mother's hand to look at a tank of crabs. He did not know his mother's phone number.
>
> I took his hand and walked him to the information counter near the entrance. The uncle there made an announcement over the loudspeaker, and I stayed with Ryan and told him about my cat so that he would stop shaking.
>
> Two minutes later, a woman came running down the aisle with her slippers slapping the wet floor. She lifted Ryan up and held him tightly for a long time.
>
> She thanked me again and again. I only nodded, because my throat felt strange.

**What makes it work:** The story is told from a clear point of view with small, believable details the pictures do not contain — the crab tank, the slapping slippers, the cat used as a distraction. Notice the kneeling detail in the second paragraph: it shows kindness through an action rather than stating "I was very kind". The last line suggests emotion without naming it, which is a step beyond the usual "I felt very happy" ending.

### 9. The Shared Umbrella  ·  *Thoughtfulness*

**The four pictures**

1. Heavy rain falls as school ends; a girl opens her umbrella
2. She sees a classmate waiting at the gate with no umbrella
3. The two of them walk home under one umbrella
4. The next day the classmate brings her a warm drink and they sit together

**Model composition**

> It rained so heavily on Tuesday afternoon that the drains outside our school were overflowing. I had brought my red umbrella, so I opened it and stepped out of the shelter feeling rather pleased with myself.
>
> Then I saw Kamala standing at the gate. She was pressed against the railing with her bag held over her head, and her shoes were already soaked. She lived in the next block to mine, but we had never once walked home together because she was quiet and I had never bothered to speak to her.
>
> I ran over and held the umbrella above both of us. It was not big enough for two, so my left shoulder was wet through by the time we reached our carpark. We talked the whole way. She was not quiet at all once somebody started.
>
> The next morning, Kamala was waiting outside my classroom with a warm Milo from the canteen. We have eaten recess together every day since.
>
> One wet shoulder, and I gained a friend I had walked past for two years.

**What makes it work:** The writer earns the last line by admitting something unflattering in the second paragraph — that she had never bothered to speak to Kamala. That admission is what turns a kind deed into a story. The soaked shoulder appears twice, first as a cost and then in the final sentence as a bargain, which gives the composition a shape. Past tense is consistent and the vocabulary is within reach of a Primary 3 writer: *overflowing, pressed, soaked, bothered*.

### 10. The Class Plant  ·  *Responsibility*

**The four pictures**

1. A boy is made plant monitor and given a watering can
2. During the long weekend he forgets all about it
3. On Tuesday the plant is drooping and its leaves are brown
4. He waters it daily and it flowers again

**Model composition**

> In January, Mr Peh made me the plant monitor of Primary Four Diligence. He handed me a green watering can and told me that the chilli plant on our window ledge was now my responsibility. I promised I would look after it and I meant it.
>
> Then came the long weekend. I spent three days playing games on my father's computer and never thought about the classroom once.
>
> When I walked in on Tuesday morning, the plant had collapsed over the side of its pot. Its leaves were brown and curled, and one of them crumbled when I touched it. Nobody scolded me, which somehow made it worse. I sat at my desk and could not look at the window.
>
> After that, I watered it every single morning before the bell. I moved the pot to the sunnier end of the ledge and pulled off the dead leaves. Six weeks later there were two small white flowers on it, and by June we had four chillies.
>
> I learnt that a promise is not something you say. It is something you do every day, even on holidays.

**What makes it work:** The second paragraph is only two sentences long, and that is deliberate — the forgetting should feel quick and careless. "Nobody scolded me, which somehow made it worse" is the kind of honest line that moves content into the top band, because it shows guilt instead of announcing it. The recovery is specific: watering, moving the pot, pulling off dead leaves, a count of flowers and chillies. The closing lesson is stated in the writer's own words rather than as a borrowed proverb.

### 11. Grandmother's Birthday Cake  ·  *Gratitude*

**The four pictures**

1. Two children count their pocket money at a table
2. They bake a cake and make a card together
3. The cake comes out lopsided and they look at it in dismay
4. Grandmother blows out the candles and hugs them both

**Model composition**

> My grandmother turned seventy in March. She had looked after my brother and me every afternoon since we were small, and we wanted to give her something she had not paid for herself.
>
> For five weeks we saved every coin we were given, hiding the tin behind the rice cooker. When we counted it on the kitchen table, we had eighteen dollars and forty cents. It was enough for flour, eggs, butter and a packet of candles.
>
> We baked the cake on Saturday while she was at the market. My brother wrote the card and I read the recipe aloud. Everything went well until we took the tin out of the oven. One side of the cake had risen and the other had not, so it leaned over like a tired old building. We stared at it in horror.
>
> We frosted it anyway and hid the worst side against the wall of the plate.
>
> When my grandmother came home and saw the candles, her eyes filled with tears. She said it was the best cake she had ever eaten, and she ate two slices to prove it.

**What makes it work:** The composition uses all four pictures but gives the third one the most space, because the lopsided cake is where the interest lies. Two details carry the whole story: the tin hidden behind the rice cooker, and hiding the worst side of the cake against the plate. The ending is moving without being sentimental, because the writer proves the claim — she ate two slices — rather than simply asserting that everybody was happy.

### 12. The Last Leg  ·  *Sportsmanship*

**The four pictures**

1. A relay race: a girl receives the baton in second place
2. The runner ahead of her trips and falls
3. She stops, helps the fallen runner up, and they finish last together
4. At assembly the principal gives her a special award while everyone claps

**Model composition**

> I was the last runner in the Primary Four relay and my class had never won anything. When Bala slapped the baton into my hand we were second, only a few steps behind Primary Four Grace, and I thought we might finally do it.
>
> Halfway down the straight, the girl in front of me caught her foot on the edge of the track and went down hard. Her baton bounced away and she did not get up.
>
> I could have run past her. The finishing line was twenty metres away and nobody would have blamed me. Instead I stopped, pulled her up by the arm, and held on while she limped the rest of the way. Both our classes crossed the line last and second last, and the cheering stopped in a strange, sudden silence.
>
> On Monday, Mr Raj called me out at assembly and gave me a Sportsmanship Award. The whole school clapped for a very long time.
>
> I still do not have a medal for running. I have something on my wall that is harder to win.

**What makes it work:** "I could have run past her" is the strongest sentence in the composition, because it shows the writer choosing rather than simply being nice. The sudden silence in the third paragraph is an observation, not a statement of feeling, and observations score better. The last two lines contrast what she lost with what she gained in eleven words, which is the kind of ending a Primary 4 marker remembers.

## A note for parents

Mark one thing at a time. A Primary 3 child told to fix their tense, add more detail, vary their sentences and improve their ending will change nothing. Pick the single weakest of the two scales, say what to do about it in one sentence, and let everything else go until next week.
