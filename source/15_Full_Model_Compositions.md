# Continuous Writing — Eight Full Model Compositions

Paper 1 Part 2 is worth **40 marks**: 20 for content and 20 for language. This is the single largest block of marks in the whole examination.

Use these the hard way. Set a timer for 46 minutes — six to plan, forty to write — and write the whole composition before you read the model. Reading the model first is comfortable and teaches you nothing.

---

## The marking bands

| Content — 20 | | Language — 20 | |
|---|---|---|---|
| 17–20 · Fully relevant, a clear and interesting plot, well-developed characters and feelings | 18 | 17–20 · Varied sentences, accurate tenses, precise vocabulary, almost no errors | 18 |
| 13–16 · Relevant with a clear plot; some development of feelings | 14 | 13–16 · Generally accurate; some variety; a few errors that do not obscure meaning | 14 |
| 9–12 · Mostly relevant; the plot is thin or rushed in places | 10 | 9–12 · Simple sentences, repeated structures, several errors | 10 |
| 5–8 · Partly relevant; events listed rather than told | 6 | 5–8 · Frequent errors; meaning sometimes unclear | 6 |
| 0–4 · Largely irrelevant to the theme or the pictures | 2 | 0–4 · Meaning often lost | 2 |

> **A word of warning about these models.** They are deliberately plain. They work by structure, specific detail and honest feeling rather than by rich description. A Singapore marking scheme also rewards vivid vocabulary, varied sentence openings and figurative language, so do not read these as permission to write flatly. Take the shape from them, and bring your own colour to it.

## 1. Honesty

**The task**

Write a composition about a time when someone owned up to something.

Your composition must be based on ONE of these three pictures: a broken window • a spilt drink on a desk • a queue at a school office.

About 150–200 words for Primary 5, 200–250 for Primary 6.

> **A plan that would work:** Setting: school office, Thursday recess. Characters: narrator, Mrs Halim. Problem: cracked a window and walked away. Climax: the assembly announcement about the science club. Resolution: owns up, is punished anyway, feels lighter.

**Model composition** (253 words)

> I had been in the school office twice before, both times to collect something. The third time was different.
>
> It began with a football and a window that cracked, rather than smashed, so quietly that nobody came running. I stood with the ball in my hands and waited for somebody to shout. Nobody did. After about ten seconds I walked away.
>
> I got through the whole of Tuesday. I was almost through Wednesday when Mrs Halim announced at assembly that a window had been damaged, that the school would not be replacing it this term, and that the science club would therefore have to move.
>
> It was the science club that did it. Not guilt exactly — something smaller and more annoying, like a stone in a shoe.
>
> At recess on Thursday I joined the queue outside the general office. There were four people ahead of me and I thought about leaving at least twice. When my turn came I said it in one breath, before I could stop myself: "I broke the window in the science corridor on Tuesday."
>
> Mrs Halim put down her pen and looked at me for a long moment.
>
> "Thank you," she said. "That will have been difficult."
>
> I had to help the cleaner for four Fridays, and my father was not pleased, and the science club still had to move. None of that was the part I remember. What I remember is walking out of that office feeling about five kilograms lighter than when I went in.

**Why it scores:** The composition does not start with the crime; it starts with the office, which is where it will end. The delay between Tuesday and Thursday is where the real story lives, and the writer gives it most of the space. "Like a stone in a shoe" does more work than a paragraph about feeling guilty would. Note that the confession is not rewarded — the punishment still happens — which is what stops the ending from being childish.

## 2. A difficult decision

**The task**

Write a composition about a difficult decision.

Your composition must be based on ONE of these three pictures: two friends arguing • a mobile phone showing a message • an empty seat on a bus.

About 150–200 words for Primary 5, 200–250 for Primary 6.

> **A plan that would work:** Setting: school, then eleven days. Characters: narrator, Aishah. Problem: asked to cover a lie. Climax: says no and loses the friendship for eleven days. Resolution: Aishah returns, almost apologises.

**Model composition** (251 words)

> Aishah and I had been friends since Primary 1. On the Tuesday of the second week of term, she asked me to lie for her.
>
> It was not a big lie. Her mother had told her to come straight home; she wanted to go to the mall with two girls from the other class. All she needed was for me to say she had been at my flat doing the Social Studies project.
>
> She held out her phone with the message already typed. All I had to do was press send from my own.
>
> "It's one message," she said. "Just say yes."
>
> I have thought since about how easy it would have been. I could have typed four words and gone back to my dinner.
>
> What stopped me was not honesty, which I would like to claim. It was that I could picture Aunty Zaleha at our door the following week, thanking my mother for having Aishah over, and my mother's face while she worked out what was happening.
>
> I said no. Aishah said, "Fine," in the way that means the opposite, and did not walk home with me for eleven days. I counted.
>
> On the twelfth day she sat down next to me at recess, opened her lunchbox, and said, "My mother found out anyway. Someone posted a photograph." Then she said, quietly, "You would have been in trouble too."
>
> That was as close to thank you as I was going to get, and I found that it was enough.

**Why it scores:** The decision is small, which makes it believable. The writer refuses to claim a noble motive — "not honesty, which I would like to claim" — and that honesty about dishonesty is what lifts the content mark. The eleven days are counted, which shows the cost. The ending gives no apology and no hug, only a sentence that means one, and the narrator accepting it.

## 3. Perseverance

**The task**

Write a composition about not giving up.

Your composition must be based on ONE of these three pictures: a swimming pool • a musical instrument • a flight of stairs.

About 150–200 words for Primary 5, 200–250 for Primary 6.

> **A plan that would work:** Setting: a third-floor flat, January to March. Characters: grandmother, narrator, father. Problem: she cannot manage the stairs. Climax: found practising alone on the second landing. Resolution: reaches the ground floor in five weeks and goes to the market alone.

**Model composition** (219 words)

> My grandmother moved in with us in January, four months after her fall, and she could not manage the stairs.
>
> We live on the third floor of a block with no lift. For the first three weeks she did not leave the flat at all. She sat by the window in the afternoons and watched the void deck, and said she was perfectly happy, which was a lie we all agreed to believe.
>
> Then one Saturday my father found her on the second landing, holding the rail with both hands, quite out of breath.
>
> "I am practising," she said.
>
> After that we practised together. Four steps on the first day. Six on the second. We went down in the morning when the stairwell was cool and back up before lunch, and at every landing she stopped and told me something — about the kampung, about my grandfather, about a teacher she had disliked in 1958 — and I understood that the stories were how she rested without admitting that she was resting.
>
> It took five weeks to get to the ground floor and back without stopping.
>
> She goes to the market by herself now, on Tuesdays and Fridays, and comes home with more than she can comfortably carry. Nobody has told her to take less. We tried that once.

**Why it scores:** The perseverance belongs to somebody else, which is a harder and more interesting choice than writing about yourself. The numbers do the work: three weeks, four steps, six steps, five weeks. The observation about the stories being disguised rest is the single best line and shows genuine inference. The last three sentences are funny and affectionate, and end the piece without a moral. One caution: this works because the prompt does not say "you". Check the wording of your own prompt first — if it asks about you, you must be at the centre of the story.

## 4. A kind act

**The task**

Write a composition about an act of kindness.

Your composition must be based on ONE of these three pictures: a coin • a hawker centre • an umbrella.

About 150–200 words for Primary 5, 200–250 for Primary 6.

> **A plan that would work:** Setting: a hawker centre, last November. Characters: narrator, Mdm Chong. Problem: sixty cents short. Climax: she leaves her stall to pay. Resolution: repayment refused, so he becomes a daily customer.

**Model composition** (216 words)

> The drinks auntie at our hawker centre is called Mdm Chong and she has never once got my order wrong in four years.
>
> Last November I went down for lunch on the day my mother's wallet had been stolen. I had exactly two dollars and forty cents, which I had counted three times on the way, and I ordered chicken rice at the stall on the end.
>
> It was three dollars.
>
> I have thought since about how long I stood there. Probably four seconds. It felt considerably longer, and I could hear the queue behind me.
>
> Then a hand came past my shoulder and put a coin on the counter, and Mdm Chong said something in Teochew to the chicken rice uncle that I did not understand, and he laughed, and the plate came.
>
> She had left her own stall unattended to do it.
>
> I tried to pay her back the next day. She refused, twice, and the second time she was annoyed, so I stopped trying. What I do instead is go to her stall for my drink every single day, even when the queue is long and the other stall is empty, and I have done that for ten months now.
>
> I do not think she has noticed. That is not really the point.

**Why it scores:** The act is tiny — sixty cents — and the writer knows it, which is why the piece spends its length on the four seconds beforehand and the ten months afterwards rather than on the coin. Leaving her own stall unattended is the detail that shows the kindness cost something. The refusal to be repaid, and the narrator's stubborn daily reply, make the ending earned rather than sentimental.

**The same story at Primary 5 length** (158 words)

> The drinks auntie at our hawker centre is Mdm Chong, and she has never got my order wrong in four years.
>
> Last November I went down for lunch on the day my mother's wallet had been stolen. I had two dollars and forty cents. The chicken rice was three dollars.
>
> I stood there for about four seconds. It felt much longer, and I could hear the queue behind me.
>
> Then a hand came past my shoulder and put a coin on the counter. Mdm Chong had left her own stall to do it.
>
> I tried to pay her back the next day. She refused twice, and the second time she was annoyed, so I stopped trying.
>
> What I do instead is buy my drink from her every single day, even when her queue is the longer one. I have done that for ten months. I do not think she has noticed, and that is not really the point.

## 5. An unexpected discovery

**The task**

Write a composition about an unexpected discovery.

Your composition must be based on ONE of these three pictures: an old box • a locked cupboard • a photograph.

About 150–200 words for Primary 5, 200–250 for Primary 6.

> **A plan that would work:** Setting: the spare room. Characters: narrator, father (in the past). Problem: finds his father's old reports. Climax: reads the Primary 4 comment. Resolution: says nothing, but understands his father differently.

**Model composition** (219 words)

> The box was under the bed in the spare room and it was full of school reports, which I did not expect to be interesting.
>
> They were my father's. Primary 1 to Secondary 4, in order, held together with a rubber band that snapped when I touched it.
>
> My father is an engineer. He checks his work twice. He has told me, more than once and in some detail, that carelessness is a choice.
>
> In Primary 4 he was ranked thirty-first out of thirty-eight.
>
> I read the whole of that report sitting on the floor of the spare room with dust on my knees. His teacher, a Mrs Pereira, had written: *Kumar is a bright boy who has decided that he is not. I would like to change his mind before he leaves this school.*
>
> Underneath, in different ink, somebody had written the date 1987 and the words *she did*.
>
> I put everything back exactly as I found it, including the broken rubber band, and I have never mentioned it.
>
> But when he tells me off about carelessness now, I listen differently. He is not describing a rule he has always kept. He is describing something that happened to him in 1987, and he is trying to pass it on, and he is not very good at saying so.

**Why it scores:** The discovery is not treasure or a secret — it is a rank on an old report, which is far more interesting. The teacher's comment is quoted in full because it is the hinge of the whole piece, and the two words in different ink do more than any amount of explanation. The final paragraph re-reads the father's behaviour in the light of the discovery, which is exactly the kind of inference a top band rewards.

**The same story at Primary 5 length** (145 words)

> The box was under the bed in the spare room, and it was full of old school reports.
>
> They were my father's, from Primary 1 to Secondary 4, held together with a rubber band that snapped when I touched it.
>
> My father is an engineer. He checks his work twice. He has told me more than once that carelessness is a choice.
>
> In Primary 4 he was ranked thirty-first out of thirty-eight.
>
> His teacher had written: *Kumar is a bright boy who has decided that he is not.*
>
> Underneath, in different ink, somebody had added the date 1987 and the words *she did*.
>
> I put everything back exactly as I had found it and have never mentioned it. But when he tells me off about carelessness now, I listen differently. He is not repeating a rule. He is passing on something that happened to him.

## 6. Facing a fear

**The task**

Write a composition about facing a fear.

Your composition must be based on ONE of these three pictures: a stage • a dog • deep water.

About 150–200 words for Primary 5, 200–250 for Primary 6.

> **A plan that would work:** Setting: school, nine days before the play. Characters: narrator, Mrs Fong, Vanessa. Problem: cast in a speaking part after two years of silence. Climax: the performance. Resolution: gets through it; the fear remains but is no longer total.

**Model composition** (242 words)

> I was cast as the narrator in the Primary 6 play, which has more lines than any other part, because Mrs Fong did not know that I had stopped speaking in class two years earlier.
>
> It was not shyness, exactly. I answered questions. I simply did not speak unless a reply was required, and after a while people stop asking, and after another while it becomes the thing everyone knows about you.
>
> I told her I could not do it. She said, "All right," and gave the part to Vanessa, and I was relieved for about four days.
>
> Then Vanessa got chickenpox.
>
> There were nine days left. Mrs Fong did not ask me again. She left the script on my desk at the end of a lesson, face down, without saying anything at all, and walked out of the room, and that was somehow much harder to refuse than a question would have been.
>
> I learnt it in five days. I was sick twice on the afternoon of the performance. My hands shook so badly during the first paragraph that the pages made a noise the microphone picked up.
>
> And then, somewhere in the second scene, I forgot to be frightened. Not bravely — I was simply too busy.
>
> I still do not enjoy speaking in front of people. But I know now that the fear does not last the whole way through, and that is a genuinely useful thing to know.

**Why it scores:** The fear is specific and has a history, which makes it real. The script left face down is the best moment in the piece precisely because nothing is said. Note the honesty of "not bravely — I was simply too busy", and the refusal of a triumphant ending: the fear is not cured, only better understood. Examiners reward that far more than "I was no longer afraid of anything".

## 7. Responsibility

**The task**

Write a composition about being trusted with something important.

Your composition must be based on ONE of these three pictures: a set of keys • a younger child • a pet.

About 150–200 words for Primary 5, 200–250 for Primary 6.

> **A plan that would work:** Setting: home, six afternoons in June. Characters: narrator, brother, mother. Problem: brother gets out of the flat. Climax: finds him on the landing below. Resolution: tells his mother; is trusted again.

**Model composition** (232 words)

> For six days in June I was in charge of my brother, who is four, from two o'clock until my mother came home at five.
>
> The first two days went perfectly, which is the dangerous part.
>
> On Wednesday I let him watch a video while I finished a chapter, and when I looked up the flat was quiet in a way that is never good. The front door was open. Not wide — about a hand's width, the way it is when somebody small has pulled it and not been able to pull it far.
>
> I have never moved faster in my life. I found him on the landing one floor down, sitting on the steps, entirely calm, waiting for the cat from 04-12 that he had apparently been visiting for three days.
>
> I did not scold him. I sat down on the step beside him and waited until my hands stopped shaking, which took a while, and then we went back upstairs and I locked the door and put the key on top of the fridge.
>
> I told my mother that evening. All of it.
>
> She was quiet for a moment, and then she said the thing I did not expect: "Thank you for telling me. You will be looking after him again on Monday."
>
> I have never once opened a book in that flat since without first checking the door.

**Why it scores:** The structure is excellent: two days that only looked easy — note that he had already been visiting the cat for three days — then the slip, then the consequence, then a resolution that is a further responsibility rather than a punishment. "Quiet in a way that is never good" and the hand's width of open door are the details that make the fear land. Not scolding the brother shows character. The mother's line is the best sentence in the piece because it is both forgiveness and a test.

## 8. Something I regret

**The task**

Write a composition about something you regret.

Your composition must be based on ONE of these three pictures: a birthday cake • an unanswered telephone • a hospital corridor.

About 150–200 words for Primary 5, 200–250 for Primary 6.

> **A plan that would work:** Setting: Sunday evenings across a year, then a hospital in November. Characters: narrator, great-aunt. Problem: avoids her weekly calls. Climax: the hospital visit, when he realises how much she had remembered. Resolution: she dies; he now answers the telephone first.

**Model composition** (224 words)

> My great-aunt telephoned our flat every Sunday evening at about seven, and for most of 2024 I did not pick up.
>
> It was not unkindness. The calls were long and slow and largely about people I had never met, and I was twelve, and there was always something else. My mother would call out that it was Ah Yee, and I would say I was doing homework, which was occasionally true.
>
> In November she went into hospital, and we visited on a Saturday, and she was smaller than I remembered and very pleased to see us.
>
> She asked me about my school, and about the recorder, which I had stopped playing in Primary 4, and about a friend called Danial whose name I had apparently mentioned to her once, years before, and forgotten I had mentioned.
>
> She had remembered all of it.
>
> I have thought about that a great deal since. She was not telephoning every Sunday to talk. She was telephoning to listen, and the reason the calls were slow was that she was giving me room, week after week, to say something — and for the better part of a year I did not.
>
> She died in February.
>
> The telephone still rings on Sunday evenings sometimes, for other reasons, and I am now the first person in our flat to reach it.

**Why it scores:** The regret is not dramatic and there is no dying apology, which is exactly why it works. The turn comes at "She had remembered all of it" — one short paragraph that re-reads the whole year. The insight that the slow calls were room being made for him is the sort of realisation that earns a top content band. The final image shows the change instead of announcing it, and the phrase "for other reasons" quietly carries the loss.
