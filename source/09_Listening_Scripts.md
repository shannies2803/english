>
> Madam Siti: The customers. When I started, almost everyone who came was from this neighbourhood. I knew their names, I knew what they ordered. Now half my customers find me on their phones, from an app. They come once, take a photograph of the food, and I never see them again.

> Interviewer: Is that a bad thing?

## Passage B — for questions 16–20

> Interviewer: You've been a school crossing warden for twenty-six years. Do you know the children by name?
>
> Mr Loke: Most of them. Not all. There are about four hundred at that school and they change every six years, so I gave up trying to know all of them a long time ago. But I know the regulars. I know who runs and who dawdles. I know which ones will step off the kerb without looking, and I position myself accordingly.
>
> Interviewer: Has anything ever gone badly wrong?
>
> Mr Loke: Once. Nineteen years ago. A car came through much faster than it should have and I had to pull a boy back by his bag. He was fine. I was not fine, for about a month. I still think about it when a car comes round that bend too quickly, which is most mornings.
>
> Interviewer: Have you ever thought of stopping?
>
> Mr Loke: Every February, when it rains for three weeks. Then it stops raining. And a child I have never spoken to says good morning to me because his older sister used to, and I think, well, another year.
>
> Interviewer: What would you want people to understand about the job?
>
> Mr Loke: That it is not about the sign. Anybody can hold a sign. It is about knowing, on a particular Tuesday, that the boy in the blue cap has had a bad morning and is not paying attention.
>

## Passage A — for questions 11–15

> Good afternoon. I'm here to explain how the Primary Six buddy reading programme will work this year, because it is a little different from last year.
>
> Each of you will be paired with a Primary One pupil. You will meet twice a week, on Tuesday and Thursday, during the second half of recess. That gives you about fifteen minutes each session. Fifteen minutes does not sound long, and it isn't, which is why preparation matters.
>
> Here is the change. Last year you chose the books. This year your buddy chooses, from a shelf of about sixty titles in the library corner. Some of you will find this frustrating. You may end up reading the same book about a truck eleven times. That is normal, and it is not a failure. Young children reread because rereading is how they build confidence.
>
> Your job is not to correct every mistake. If your buddy says a word wrongly but the sentence still makes sense, let it go. If the sentence stops making sense, wait three seconds, then help. Three seconds feels like a very long time. Count it in your head.
>
> Finally, keep the record card. One line per session: the title, and one thing your buddy did well. Not what they got wrong. One thing they did well. Hand it in at the end of term.
>

## Passage B — for questions 16–20

> Interviewer: You've repaired shoes on this street for forty-one years. Is the trade dying?
>
> Mr Teo: Everybody asks me that. The honest answer is that it changed, and then it changed back a little.
>
> Interviewer: Changed back?
>
> Mr Teo: In the nineties, nobody repaired anything. A shoe broke, you bought another shoe. My busiest years were the sixties and seventies, and then it went very quiet for about twenty years. I nearly closed twice.
>
> Interviewer: And now?
>
> Mr Teo: Now I have young customers. Twenty-five, thirty years old. They bring me expensive boots and they want them to last. Some of them ask me to teach them. I have taught four people, and three of them gave up within a month, which is about the usual rate.
>
> Interviewer: Why do they give up?
>
> Mr Teo: Because they expect to be good at it. Nobody is good at it. I was not good at it for six years. My teacher let me resole practice shoes that were going to be thrown away anyway, and he never once told me I was doing well, because I was not.
>
> Interviewer: That sounds harsh.
>
> Mr Teo: It was accurate. There is a difference. When I finally did something properly he said one word, and I have remembered that word for forty years. If he had said it every week it would have been worth nothing.
>

## Passage B — for questions 16–20

> Interviewer: You have been a swimming coach for thirty-four years. What have you changed your mind about?
>
> Coach Devi: Quite a lot. When I started, I believed that the child who wanted it most would win. I was completely wrong.
>
> Interviewer: Wrong how?
>
> Coach Devi: Wanting it is not rare. Nearly every child in that pool at six in the morning wants it. What is rare is being able to lose a race on Saturday and still be in the water on Monday. That is the thing I cannot teach, and it is the thing that decides almost everything.
>
> Interviewer: Can you spot it early?
>
> Coach Devi: Not from the swimming. I watch what happens in the ten minutes after a bad race. Some of them want to be told it was not their fault. A few go and look at the board to see the actual time. Those are the ones.
>
> Interviewer: What do you say to a child who has just swum badly?
>
> Coach Devi: As little as possible on the day. They are not able to hear it. I tell them one specific thing that was good, because there is always one, and then I leave them alone. We talk properly on Monday.
>
> Interviewer: And the parents?
>
> Coach Devi: The parents are usually harder than the children. A parent who says nothing in the car is worth more than any coach. I have told a great many of them that, and about half of them have listened.
>
