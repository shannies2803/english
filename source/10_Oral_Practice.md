# Oral Practice — Paper 4 (30 marks)

Reading Aloud is worth 10 marks and Stimulus-based Conversation 20. Both are practised out loud. Reading silently will not prepare a child for either.

---

## Part 1 — Reading Aloud

**How to use these:** give the child five minutes with the passage. They should read it silently once for meaning, mark the pauses, circle difficult words, and underline the words to stress. Then they read it aloud to you. Read the notes yourself first — they list what to listen for.

### Passage 1  ·  *P4 and above*

> Every morning before school, my grandfather waters the plants along our corridor. There are eleven pots altogether, and he knows exactly how much water each one needs. The chilli plant, he says, is the fussiest of the lot. If you give it too much, the leaves turn yellow within a week. Last month, he taught me how to check the soil with my finger before pouring. I get it wrong about half the time, but he never seems to mind. He simply says that plants are patient teachers, and that they will wait for me to learn.

**Listen for:**

- Pause slightly at every comma; take a full breath at each full stop.
- Stress the numbers: **eleven** pots, **half** the time.
- Watch the final consonants: waters, needs, leaves, plants, teachers.
- 'Fussiest' has three syllables — FUSS-i-est, not 'fussest'.
- Let your voice fall on the last sentence. It is the point of the passage.

### Passage 2  ·  *P5 and above*

> The old cinema on the corner closed down last year. For as long as I can remember, its red sign had glowed above the traffic, spelling out the name of whatever film was showing that week. My mother watched her first film there when she was nine. She says the seats squeaked terribly and the air conditioning was far too cold, and that she loved every minute of it. Now the building is boarded up, and somebody has stuck a notice on the door announcing that a supermarket will open in its place. My mother walked past it with me last Sunday. She did not say anything at all, which told me more than any complaint would have.

**Listen for:**

- 'Cinema' — CIN-e-ma, three syllables.
- Slow down for the list in sentence four; the two 'and's need a small pause before each.
- Stress 'nine' — the age matters to the story.
- 'Boarded up' — sound the d clearly.
- The last sentence should be read slowly and quietly. It carries the feeling.

### Passage 3  ·  *P6 and above*

> Singapore's first public library opened in 1845 with just a few hundred volumes, most of them donated. Today the network holds more than eight million items and lends out over thirty million a year. What has changed is not only the size of the collection but the purpose of the building itself. A library is no longer only a place to borrow books. It is a place where students revise for examinations, where retirees read newspapers in five languages, and where a child might attend her first storytelling session. The shelves matter, of course. But increasingly, what matters more is the space between them.

**Listen for:**

- Read the numbers clearly and slightly slower: eighteen forty-five, eight million, thirty million.
- 'Volumes' here means books — read it with confidence, not hesitation.
- The three 'where' clauses form a list. Keep the same rhythm for each and pause briefly between them.
- Stress 'not only' and 'but'.
- Final sentence: slight pause after 'But increasingly'. Land firmly on 'between them'.

### Passage 4  ·  *P6 and above*

> There is a particular sound a badminton shuttle makes when it is struck cleanly, and anybody who has played the game will recognise it at once. It is not loud. It is a short, dry click, quite unlike the dull thud of a mishit. My coach used to say that she could mark our technique with her eyes closed, simply by listening from the far end of the hall. At the time I assumed she was exaggerating. Years later, standing at the back of a school hall during a training session, I found that I could do exactly the same thing.

**Listen for:**

- 'Shuttle', 'struck', 'cleanly' — crisp consonant clusters, do not rush them.
- 'A short, dry click' — pause at each comma. The rhythm imitates the sound.
- Contrast 'click' with 'dull thud' in your tone: light, then heavy.
- 'Exaggerating' — five syllables, eg-ZAJ-er-ay-ting.
- The last sentence is a quiet realisation. Do not raise your voice for it.

### Passage 5  ·  *P4 and above*

> The lift in our block has been broken twice this month. The first time, it stopped between the fourth and fifth floors with three people inside, including Mrs Osman from the ninth floor. She told us afterwards that she had not been frightened at all, which nobody believed, and that she had spent the twenty minutes teaching the other two passengers a card game. The engineers say the motor is old and needs replacing. Until then, we take the stairs and grumble, and Mrs Osman takes the stairs and does not.

**Listen for:**

- 'Osman' — OSS-man, stress the first syllable.
- Pause at each comma in the long third sentence; it has three parts.
- Stress 'twice', 'three' and 'twenty' — the numbers carry the story.
- Final consonants: stopped, believed, replacing, grumble.
- The last sentence is the joke. Slow down slightly and let the contrast land.

### Passage 6  ·  *P5 and above*

> Every Thursday my grandmother cooks for eleven people, although only four of us live in the house. The rest of the food is packed into containers and delivered, by me, to three flats on our floor. She has been doing this since before I was born and has never once described it as charity. When I asked her why she cooks so much, she looked at me as though I had asked why she breathes, and said that a pot is a pot and the difference between four and eleven is one more handful of rice.

**Listen for:**

- 'Although' — do not swallow the first syllable.
- Pause after 'delivered, by me,' — the phrase is deliberately interrupted.
- Stress 'eleven' and 'four' both times; the numbers are the point.
- 'Charity' — CHA-ri-ty, three syllables.
- The final sentence is long. Breathe after 'breathes' and finish steadily.

### Passage 7  ·  *P6 and above*

> The Singapore Botanic Gardens received its UNESCO listing in 2015, more than a century and a half after it was first laid out. What strikes most visitors is not the rarest orchid or the tallest tree, but the ordinary Saturday use of the place: families spreading mats on the lawn, students revising under the rain trees, an elderly couple walking the same loop they have walked for decades. A garden that is only admired becomes a museum. A garden that is used becomes part of a city's daily life, and that, arguably, is the harder thing to achieve.

**Listen for:**

- 'UNESCO' — read it as a word, you-NESS-ko, not letter by letter.
- '2015' — twenty fifteen.
- The list in the middle has three items. Keep the same rhythm and pause between them.
- Contrast the two short sentences near the end: drop your voice slightly on 'museum', lift it on 'daily life'.
- 'Arguably' — AR-gyoo-ably, four syllables. Do not rush it.

### Passage 8  ·  *P6 and above*

> There is a particular silence in a hall thirty seconds before a performance begins. It is not the absence of sound — somebody is always coughing, a chair always creaks — but the absence of anybody wanting to make any. I have felt it from both sides now, as a performer waiting in the wings and as a parent sitting eleven rows back, and I could not honestly tell you which is worse. What I do know is that the silence ends the instant the first note is played, and that everyone in the room, without exception, breathes out.

**Listen for:**

- 'Particular' — four syllables, par-TIC-u-lar.
- The dashes mark an interruption. Drop your pitch slightly for 'somebody is always coughing, a chair always creaks' and come back up.
- Stress 'both' in 'from both sides now'.
- 'Without exception' — separate the two words clearly; do not run them together.
- End on 'breathes out' gently. It is a quiet realisation, not an announcement.

### Passage 9  ·  *P4 and above*

> My father has kept the same wooden toolbox for twenty-three years. The handle is worn smooth, one hinge is held on with wire, and inside there is a jumble of screwdrivers, a hammer with a chipped head and a tin of nails that he refuses to throw away. Last month my mother bought him a new plastic toolbox for his birthday, with neat compartments and a lock. He thanked her warmly, admired it for a while, and then carried it into the store room. The old one is still by the door, where he can reach it.

**Listen for:**

- 'twenty-three' — keep both parts clear; do not swallow the 'three'.
- The list in the second sentence has four items. Pause slightly at each comma so the list can be heard.
- Final consonants: kept, worn, chipped, refuses, thanked, admired, carried.
- Stress 'new' in the third sentence and 'old' in the last one — the contrast is the point of the passage.
- The last sentence is the punchline. Slow down and let 'where he can reach it' land.

### Passage 10  ·  *P5 and above*

> Hawker centres were not built for food. They were built for order. In the nineteen sixties, thousands of street hawkers cooked over open fires along the five-foot ways of Singapore, and the government wanted them licensed, plumbed and inspected. So it put up halls with concrete floors and numbered stalls, and moved everybody indoors. Nobody at the time imagined that these plain buildings would one day be listed by UNESCO, or that queues would form at seven in the morning for a bowl of noodles cooked by a man in his eighties who has never changed his recipe.

**Listen for:**

- Open with a firm, almost flat delivery on the first two sentences — they are a surprising claim and work best when stated plainly.
- 'nineteen sixties', 'five-foot ways', 'UNESCO' — say each of these slowly; they are the hard ones.
- 'licensed, plumbed and inspected' — three past participles in a row. Sound the final -ed on each.
- Pause after 'So it put up halls with concrete floors and numbered stalls,' before 'and moved everybody indoors'.
- Lift your voice on 'never changed his recipe' at the end — it carries the admiration.

### Passage 11  ·  *P6 and above*

> The first person to swim across the Straits of Johor for charity did it in nineteen eighty-two, and she was fifty-six years old. She had learnt to swim at the age of forty-nine, in a public pool, from a teacher who charged her nothing. When reporters asked what the hardest part had been, they expected her to say the current, or the cold, or the jellyfish. She said it was the first morning at the pool, standing at the shallow end in a borrowed swimming costume while children half her height swam past her. Everything after that, she said, was merely water.

**Listen for:**

- Say 'nineteen eighty-two' and 'fifty-six' distinctly — the two numbers are the hook of the passage.
- 'the current, or the cold, or the jellyfish' — a list of three. Give each one equal weight and pause before the last.
- The fourth sentence is long. Breathe after 'shallow end' and again after 'costume'.
- Stress 'first morning' — it answers the reporters' question and overturns their expectation.
- 'was merely water' — drop your voice and slow right down. Do not rush the final three words.

### Passage 12  ·  *P6 and above*

> Consider the humble queue. It is one of the few things in public life that works entirely on agreement: there is no law compelling you to stand behind the person who arrived before you, and yet almost everybody does. Watch what happens when somebody steps in near the front. Nobody shouts. Instead there is a ripple of glances, a shifting of bags, a pointed clearing of the throat, and very often the offender retreats without a word being spoken to them. It is a remarkably efficient system, held together by nothing more solid than the fear of being looked at.

**Listen for:**

- Open conversationally on 'Consider the humble queue' — you are inviting the listener to notice something.
- 'compelling', 'offender', 'remarkably', 'efficient' — practise these four aloud before you begin.
- The list in the fourth sentence builds: glances, bags, throat. Let each one get slightly more emphasis.
- Pause after 'Nobody shouts.' It is a short sentence on purpose, and the pause does the work.
- Land firmly on 'the fear of being looked at' — this is the joke and the point at the same time.

### Passage 13  ·  *P3 and above*

> Our class has a tortoise called Bruce. He is older than everybody in the room, including Mrs Ong. He moves so slowly that we once thought he had stopped moving altogether, and Faizal nearly cried. Then Bruce turned his head, very slightly, and looked at us as though we had been extremely rude. He eats cabbage on Thursdays, sleeps most of the rest of the week, and has never once done anything exciting. He is the most popular member of our class.

**Listen for:**

- 'Bruce' — one clear syllable. Do not rush past it; the name is part of the joke.
- Pause at the commas in the fifth sentence (“He eats cabbage on Thursdays…”). It has three parts.
- Final consonants: moves, stopped, turned, looked, eats, sleeps.
- Stress 'never once' and 'most popular' — the contrast is the point of the passage.
- Slow down for the last sentence and let it land. Do not smile it away.

### Passage 14  ·  *P4 and above*

> There is a bird in Singapore that can copy almost any sound it hears. The common mynah has been recorded imitating car alarms, mobile phone ringtones and, in one case, the whistle a man used to call his dog. The dog, it is said, came running three times before anybody worked out what was happening. Scientists think mynahs copy sounds to mark out territory. Whatever the reason, it means that some of the noises you hear outside your window in the morning were not made by a machine at all.

**Listen for:**

- 'mynah' — MY-na. Say it clearly the first time; it is the key word.
- The list in the second sentence has three items. Pause before 'and, in one case'.
- Final consonants: recorded, imitating, worked, mark, machine.
- Stress 'three times' — it is the funny detail.
- The last sentence is a mild surprise. Slow slightly on 'not made by a machine at all'.

### Passage 15  ·  *P4 and above*

> My mother keeps a list on the fridge of everything she has ever lent to a neighbour. There are fourteen items on it. Next to eleven of them she has written a date, a name and a small tick. Next to the other three she has written a date and a name and nothing else, and she has been writing nothing else beside those three for about six years. She says she is not annoyed. She says she keeps the list only so that she remembers what she owns. Nobody in our family believes this, and nobody says so.

**Listen for:**

- Numbers carry this passage: 'fourteen', 'eleven', 'three', 'six years'. Say each one clearly.
- Pause after 'a date, a name and a small tick' before the next sentence.
- Do not rush 'and she has been writing nothing else beside those three for about six years'. Breathe after 'three'.
- The two sentences beginning 'She says' should sound flat and innocent. That is where the humour is.
- Land firmly on the last sentence, and pause a beat before 'and nobody says so'.

### Passage 16  ·  *P5 and above*

> The lift in my grandmother's block has a mirror on the back wall, and for about a year it was cracked right across the middle. Everybody complained. Somebody wrote to the town council twice. Then one Tuesday it was replaced, and within a week three different neighbours had told my grandmother that the lift felt bigger. It is exactly the same lift. Nothing about it changed except that you can now see the whole of yourself in it, which I find a strange and rather cheerful thing to know about people.

**Listen for:**

- 'grandmother's' — sound the possessive s; do not let it run into 'block'.
- Pause at each full stop in the short middle sentences. They are short on purpose.
- Final consonants: cracked, complained, replaced, changed.
- Stress 'exactly the same lift' — the contrast is the whole point of the passage.
- Slow down for the last clause and let 'cheerful' sound warm rather than clever.

### Passage 17  ·  *P5 and above*

> A hawker in Chinatown has sold the same three dishes for thirty-one years and has never put a photograph on his stall. When a food blogger asked him why, he said that a photograph tells you what a dish looks like, which is the least important thing about it. He then refused to be photographed himself. The queue that morning was eleven people long, and nobody in it was holding a phone up, which may have been the point he was making all along.

**Listen for:**

- 'thirty-one' — keep both parts clear.
- The second sentence is long. Breathe after 'why', and again after 'looks like'.
- Final consonants: sold, asked, refused, holding.
- Stress 'least important' — it is the surprising part of what he says.
- The last clause is dry. Do not over-emphasise it; let it sit.

### Passage 18  ·  *P6 and above*

> There is a kind of silence peculiar to a school hall in the minute before a performance begins. It is not quiet — four hundred people cannot be quiet — but it is attentive, which is a different thing altogether. Chairs stop scraping. Somebody's grandmother is still unwrapping a sweet and everybody can hear her doing it. Then the lights drop, and the sound that follows is the only applause of the evening that is given for nothing at all, before a single note has been played.

**Listen for:**

- 'peculiar' — pe-CUL-iar, three syllables, stress the second.
- The dashes in the second sentence are pauses. Lift your voice slightly inside them.
- Final consonants: begins, scraping, unwrapping, played.
- 'the only applause of the evening that is given for nothing at all' — do not rush this; it is the idea the whole passage is built towards.
- End quietly. The last five words should be almost spoken rather than read.

### Passage 19  ·  *P3 and above*

> My little brother collects bus tickets. He has a shoebox full of them under his bed, and he knows where every single one came from. Last week he found an old ticket on the pavement outside our block. It was wet and the printing had gone, so nobody could tell which bus it was from. He kept it anyway. He says it is the most interesting one, because it is the only one that is a mystery.

**Listen for:**

- Short, simple sentences. Pause fully at each full stop and do not run them together.
- 'shoebox' — one word, two clear parts.
- Final consonants: collects, knows, found, kept, says.
- Stress 'anyway' — it is the surprise in the story.
- Smile slightly on the last sentence. It is meant to be funny and fond.

## Part 2 — Stimulus-based Conversation

Three questions follow each stimulus. The child looks at the stimulus for a short while, then answers aloud. Model answers are given — they are one good version, not the only one. What matters is the **shape**: Point, Elaborate, Example, Link.

> **The single most common mistake** is a one-sentence answer. Four to six sentences is the target for every question.

### Screen time  ·  *P5 and above*

*(The stimulus for this topic is shown in the app under Practice. A description is given below so the conversation can also be done from this document.)*

**Stimulus summary:**   HEALTH PROMOTION BOARD  How much is too much?    4h 12m  average daily screen time, Singapore children aged 7&ndash;12    2h  recommended daily limit outside schoolwork    Children who spend more than three hours a day on screens are more likely to report poor sleep, eye strain and difficulty concentrating in class.   Try this:  no screens in the bedroom · a device-free dinner table · one screen-free day each week  

**Q1. What are your thoughts on the information in this poster?**

> I find the difference between the two figures quite striking. The poster says children my age spend over four hours a day on screens, which is double the recommended limit of two hours. Personally, I think this is a fair reflection of what I see around me — during the holidays, several of my classmates spend most of the afternoon gaming or watching videos. What worries me most is the point about concentration, because that affects schoolwork without anyone noticing the cause. I do think the poster is a little simplistic, though, since not all screen time is the same: doing research for a project is different from scrolling through videos.

**Q2. Tell me about a time when you spent too long on a screen. What happened?**

> During the December holidays last year, I started watching a series on a Saturday afternoon and did not stop until nearly midnight. I had told myself I would watch two episodes. The next morning I woke up at eleven with a headache, and I felt irritable for most of the day. Worse, I had promised my mother I would help her clear the storeroom that morning, and I was in no state to do it. She did not scold me, which somehow made it worse. Since then I have started setting an alarm before I begin watching anything, which helps more than I expected.

**Q3. Do you think schools should teach pupils about managing screen time?**

> Yes, I believe they should, and I would go further than a single assembly talk. This is because most of us know that too much screen time is bad for us, but knowing is not the difficulty — managing the habit is. For instance, my school held a talk last year with very similar statistics to this poster, and nothing changed afterwards. What might work better is a practical activity, such as tracking our own usage for a week and then discussing the results in class, because seeing your own number is far more convincing than seeing an average. Overall, I think schools are the right place for this, provided the lesson is practical rather than just informative.

### Neighbourhood volunteering  ·  *P6 and above*

*(The stimulus for this topic is shown in the app under Practice. A description is given below so the conversation can also be done from this document.)*

**Stimulus summary:**   SENGKANG RESIDENTS' NETWORK  Two hours. One Saturday. Your block.  We are looking for 40 volunteers aged 10 and above.    Meal delivery  Bring hot meals to residents who cannot leave their flats.   Reading buddies  Read with children at the study corner.   Garden plot  Water, weed and harvest the community garden.   Tech help  Help older residents use their phones.   Last year, 12 of our 40 volunteers came back for a second session. We would like that number to be higher. Tell us what would make you return.  

**Q1. Which of these activities would you choose, and why?**

> I would choose the reading buddies role. This is because I have a younger cousin whom I read with most weekends, so I already have some idea of how to keep a child interested when the book gets difficult. I also think it is the role where a primary school pupil can genuinely be useful rather than just present — a younger child may find it easier to read aloud to someone close to their own age than to an adult. Tech help appeals to me too, but I suspect I would be less patient than the job requires.

**Q2. The poster says only 12 out of 40 volunteers came back. Why do you think that is?**

> I think there are a few reasons. Firstly, people often sign up for a one-off event without intending it to become a commitment, especially if a school or a parent encouraged them. Secondly, if volunteers are not told what difference they made, the experience can feel forgettable. For instance, when my class collected donations last year, we never found out what happened to the money, and I noticed that fewer people volunteered the second time. Finally, two hours on a Saturday competes with tuition and family time, which is a real obstacle for children my age. I would suggest the organisers send a short follow-up message telling volunteers exactly what their work achieved.

**Q3. Some people say children are too young to volunteer. What do you think?**

> I disagree, although I understand the concern. The worry is presumably that children may get in the way or need supervising themselves, and for some tasks that is fair — I would not expect a ten-year-old to deliver meals alone to a stranger's flat. However, there are many roles where being young is actually an advantage. Reading with a small child is one. Helping an elderly resident with a phone is another, because most of us grew up with these devices and can explain them simply. In my view the question is not whether children are too young, but whether the task has been matched properly to the volunteer.

### Food waste at the school canteen  ·  *P6 and above*

*(The stimulus for this topic is shown in the app under Practice. A description is given below so the conversation can also be done from this document.)*

**Stimulus summary:**   RIVERBANK PRIMARY · CANTEEN AUDIT  What ends up in the bin  Food thrown away per week, by stall    Rice &amp; noodles  Mixed veg rice  Drinks stall  Snacks    48 kg   32 kg   8 kg   5 kg   Most of the waste is rice left on plates, not unsold food. Pupils were asked why. The two most common answers were  “the portion was too big”  and  “I didn't have time to finish” .  

**Q1. What does this audit tell you about food waste in your canteen?**

> The chart shows that the rice and noodle stall throws away by far the most, at forty-eight kilograms a week, which is nearly ten times the snack stall. What I find most revealing is the note underneath: the waste is mainly rice left on plates rather than food that went unsold. That means the problem is not the stallholders ordering too much, but what happens after the food is served. The two reasons pupils gave — portions being too large and not having enough time — suggest this is more about how recess is organised than about pupils being careless.

**Q2. Have you ever thrown away food at school? Why?**

> Yes, more often than I would like to admit. Last term I frequently left half my rice, mostly because recess is only twenty-five minutes and I would spend the first ten queueing. By the time I sat down I had about eight minutes to eat, and if my friends finished first I would give up and follow them out. Looking back, the food itself was fine — I simply did not allow myself time. Since then I have started going to the shorter queue even when it is not my favourite stall, which has helped.

**Q3. What one change would you suggest to reduce this waste?**

> I would suggest offering a smaller rice portion at a slightly lower price, and making pupils choose the size when they order. This is because the audit points directly at portion size as one of the two main reasons, and it is the easier of the two to fix — lengthening recess would need the whole timetable to change. A smaller option also rewards pupils for choosing it, rather than lecturing them. For instance, my cousin's school introduced a 'small rice' option last year and the canteen reported noticeably less waste within a month. Overall, I think practical changes work better than posters telling us not to waste food.

### Learning a musical instrument  ·  *P5 and above*

*(The stimulus for this topic is shown in the app under Practice. A description is given below so the conversation can also be done from this document.)*

**Stimulus summary:**   NATIONAL ARTS COUNCIL · YOUTH SURVEY  Why children stop playing    Too little time  Practice is boring  Not improving  Exam pressure    63%   48%   37%   30%   Of children who began an instrument before age ten,  54%  had stopped by age fourteen.  Among those who continued, the most common reason given was  “I play with other people.”   

**Q1. What do you think of the information in this survey?**

> What surprises me most is that more than half of the children who start an instrument have given up by fourteen. I would have guessed it was high, but not that high. Looking at the reasons, I notice that the top two — not enough time and practice being boring — are really about how practice feels day to day, rather than about the instrument itself. I also think the last line is the most interesting part of the whole survey: the children who kept going said it was because they played with other people. That suggests the problem may be less about discipline and more about doing it alone in a room.

**Q2. Tell me about something you learnt that was difficult at first.**

> I started learning the guzheng in Primary Three and I very nearly stopped in the first year. The finger picks kept slipping and my playing sounded nothing like my teacher's, which was discouraging. What changed was joining the school ensemble in Primary Four. Suddenly I was one part of a sound rather than the whole of it, and even when my own part was imperfect the music still worked. I practised more after that, not because anyone told me to, but because I did not want to be the one letting the group down. I have been playing for four years now.

**Q3. Should every child be required to learn an instrument in school?**

> I agree to a certain extent, though I would not make it compulsory for years on end. This is because there is real value in every child discovering whether they enjoy music, and many would never find out otherwise, particularly if instruments are expensive at home. However, forcing a child to continue for six years when they clearly dislike it seems likely to produce exactly the outcome this survey describes. What I would suggest is a compulsory year of group music in lower primary, followed by a genuine choice. That way everyone gets the chance, and the ones who continue are continuing because they want to, which the survey suggests is what makes people stay.

### Helping elderly neighbours  ·  *P6 and above*

*(The stimulus for this topic is shown in the app under Practice. A description is given below so the conversation can also be done from this document.)*

**Stimulus summary:**   SILVER GENERATION OFFICE  One in four lives alone  Residents aged 65 and above in our estate    26%  live alone   41%  say they speak to no one on a typical weekday   12%  have difficulty using a smartphone for appointments   3 in 5  say they would rather not be visited by strangers   The office is recruiting volunteers for weekly check-ins. Training is provided. Volunteers must be 13 or above, or accompanied by an adult.  

**Q1. Which figure on this poster stands out most to you, and why?**

> The one that strikes me is that forty-one per cent say they speak to no one on a typical weekday. The figure about living alone is sad but expected; going a whole day without a single conversation is something I find much harder to imagine. What makes it more difficult is the last box, which says three in five would rather not be visited by strangers. So the people who most need company are also the ones least likely to accept it from someone they do not know. That seems to me the real problem the poster is pointing at, even though it does not say so directly.

**Q2. Have you ever helped an older person? What happened?**

> My grandmother's neighbour, Mr Chia, is eighty-one and lives by himself two doors down. Last year his phone stopped receiving his clinic appointment reminders and he missed one, which upset him a great deal. I spent about half an hour with him going through the settings, and then I wrote the steps out on a piece of card in large handwriting and taped it inside his cupboard door. What I learnt was that the writing-down part mattered more than the fixing part, because he could then do it himself the next time. He has not needed to ask me again.

**Q3. What could a school do to help elderly residents nearby?**

> I think a school is actually well placed to help, but it has to be done carefully given that most elderly residents do not want strangers at the door. My suggestion would be a regular activity at the community centre rather than home visits — for example, a fortnightly session where pupils help with phones and appointment apps, with the same pupils returning each time. Familiarity is the key point, because after three or four sessions we would no longer be strangers. I would also keep it practical rather than purely social, since asking for help with a phone feels easier for many people than admitting to being lonely.

### Reading for pleasure  ·  *P6 and above*

*(The stimulus for this topic is shown in the app under Practice. A description is given below so the conversation can also be done from this document.)*

**Stimulus summary:**   NATIONAL LIBRARY BOARD · READING HABITS  Minutes read per day, age 10&ndash;12  Excluding schoolwork    2015  2020  2025    34 min   22 min   15 min   Children who read for pleasure at least twenty minutes a day scored, on average,  nine months ahead  in reading age compared with those who read under five minutes.  The most common reason given for not reading:  “I can&rsquo;t find a book I like.”   

**Q1. What does this chart tell you, and does it worry you?**

> The chart shows that daily reading for pleasure has fallen from thirty-four minutes in 2015 to fifteen in 2025, so it has dropped by more than half in ten years. It does concern me, mainly because of the second figure underneath: children who read at least twenty minutes a day are around nine months ahead in reading age. That means the gap is not only about enjoyment but about ability. What I find slightly more hopeful is the last line, because 'I can't find a book I like' is a solvable problem, unlike simply not wanting to read at all.

**Q2. What do you read, and how did you find it?**

> I mostly read mysteries and non-fiction about animals. I came to mysteries entirely by accident — my aunt gave me a detective novel two Christmases ago that I assumed I would hate, and I finished it in three days. Since then I have read eleven books in the same series. What I have noticed is that I almost never find books by browsing; I find them because somebody who knows me hands me one. The library has thousands of books and I usually leave empty-handed, but a single recommendation from a friend works almost every time.

**Q3. How would you get more children in your school reading?**

> I would focus on the reason the poster gives, which is that children cannot find a book they like. Telling people to read more does not solve that. My suggestion would be a shelf at the back of each classroom where pupils leave a book they loved with a handwritten note explaining why, signed with their name. This would work because a recommendation from someone in your own class carries far more weight than a poster or a teacher's list. For instance, everyone in my class read the same graphic novel last year purely because one boy would not stop talking about it. Overall, I think the answer is matching children to books, not asking them to try harder.

### Sleep and school start times  ·  *P5 and above*

*(The stimulus for this topic is shown in the app under Practice. A description is given below so the conversation can also be done from this document.)*

**Stimulus summary:**   HEALTH PROMOTION BOARD · PUPIL SURVEY  How much sleep do we actually get?    Primary 1–2  Primary 3–4  Primary 5–6  Secondary 1–2    9h 24m   8h 54m   8h 06m   7h 12m   Doctors recommend  9 to 11 hours  a night for children aged 6 to 13. Pupils in the survey who slept fewer than 8 hours were  twice as likely  to report falling asleep in class. The most common reason given for a late bedtime was not homework but  screens in the bedroom .  

**Q1. What do you find most interesting about the information in this survey?**

> The part I find most interesting is that the amount of sleep drops steadily as pupils get older, when you might expect the opposite, because older children have more homework but are also better at organising themselves. What struck me even more was the reason given at the bottom. I had assumed homework was the main cause of late bedtimes, so learning that screens in the bedroom mattered more than homework was surprising. It suggests the problem is not that children have too much to do, but that we find it very hard to stop once we have started watching something.

**Q2. Tell me about your own bedtime routine. Is it something you would change?**

> On school nights I am supposed to be in bed by half past nine, and most of the time I am. What actually happens is that I lie there with my tablet for another twenty minutes, so I fall asleep closer to ten. I would change one thing: I would leave the tablet charging in the living room instead of on my bedside table. My mother suggested that months ago and I resisted, but looking at this survey I can see she was right. The nights I sleep best are the ones when I have left it outside, usually because I forgot it there.

**Q3. Some people say primary schools should start later in the morning. What do you think?**

> I agree to a certain extent, though I do not think it would solve the problem on its own. Starting at eight instead of half past seven would certainly help pupils who travel a long way, because some of my classmates leave home before six. However, I suspect that many children would simply sleep later rather than sleep longer, since the real cause according to this survey is what happens at bedtime, not what happens in the morning. What I would suggest instead is a later start twice a week, perhaps on the two days with the heaviest lessons, together with a rule that phones and tablets are charged outside the bedroom. That way we would be tackling both ends of the night rather than just one.

### Learning to cook at home  ·  *P4 and above*

*(The stimulus for this topic is shown in the app under Practice. A description is given below so the conversation can also be done from this document.)*

**Stimulus summary:**   SCHOOL NEWSLETTER · HOME SKILLS  Can you cook one meal on your own?    Boil an egg  Cook rice  Fry an egg  Cook a vegetable dish    71%   54%   38%   19%   In a survey of 480 Primary 4 to 6 pupils,  62%  said they had never cooked anything using a stove. The most common thing pupils could prepare without help was instant noodles. Of the pupils who cooked regularly at home, almost all said they had learnt from  a grandparent , not from a lesson or a video.  

**Q1. What can you tell me about the information in this newsletter?**

> The newsletter shows that most pupils in my level cannot cook very much. More than seven in ten can boil an egg, but fewer than two in ten can cook a vegetable dish, which needs cutting, heating oil and judging when something is done. I also noticed that nearly two thirds have never used a stove at all. The last sentence is the one I keep thinking about, though. The pupils who can cook learnt it from a grandparent rather than from a video, which tells me this is a skill that gets passed on by standing next to someone, not by watching a screen.

**Q2. Have you ever helped to prepare a meal? Tell me what happened.**

> Yes, I have. Last year my mother taught me to make tomato and egg, which is the first dish she ever learnt herself. The first time, I put the eggs in before the pan was hot enough and they turned into a grey lump that stuck to the bottom. She made me scrub the pan and then let me try again the same evening, which I did not enjoy at the time. Now I can make it properly and I cook it on Saturdays when my parents are both working. My younger brother says it is better than my mother's, but he says that only when she is in the room.

**Q3. Do you think cooking should be taught in primary school? Why?**

> I strongly believe it should, because cooking is one of the few subjects that every single person will use for the rest of their life, whatever job they end up doing. Knowing how to cook simple food also makes people healthier, since someone who can prepare a vegetable dish in fifteen minutes is far less likely to buy fried food every day. I know that schools worry about safety with stoves and knives, and that is a fair concern, so I would begin with dishes that need no heat at all in the lower primary levels, such as sandwiches and salads, and move on to the stove only in Primary 5 and 6 in small groups. Even a single term of lessons would mean that nobody leaves primary school able to cook only instant noodles.

### Litter in the neighbourhood  ·  *P6 and above*

*(The stimulus for this topic is shown in the app under Practice. A description is given below so the conversation can also be done from this document.)*

**Stimulus summary:**   TOWN COUNCIL · CLEANLINESS REPORT  Who actually picks it up?    Drink cups and cans  Cigarette ends  Food packaging  Tissue paper    34%   27%   23%   16%   Our town council employs  38 cleaners  to sweep the estate every day. A one-week count found that  82%  of the litter collected was dropped within  ten metres of a bin . In a trial, painting bright footprints on the ground leading to the bins reduced litter in that block by  a third , without any fines being issued.  

**Q1. What do you think is the most important point in this report?**

> For me the most important figure is that 82% of the litter was dropped within ten metres of a bin. That changes how I understand the problem completely. If the litter were far from any bin, the answer would be to build more bins, but it is not, so the issue is not convenience at all. People walk past a bin and drop something anyway. The footprint trial at the end supports that reading, because painting footprints does not make the bins any closer or any emptier. It only makes people notice them, and that alone cut the litter by a third without a single fine.

**Q2. Tell me about a time you saw someone drop litter. What did you do?**

> A few months ago I was walking home from the market with my father when a man ahead of us finished a drink and left the plastic cup on top of an electrical box beside the lift lobby. There was a bin at the foot of the block, perhaps five steps away. I did not say anything to him, partly because I was nervous and partly because my father had already put his hand on my shoulder. After the man had gone into the lift, my father picked the cup up and threw it away, and he said one thing: that somebody always clears it, and it is never the person who left it. I have thought about that sentence many times since, and it is why I now pick things up on my own floor.

**Q3. What would you do to keep your neighbourhood clean, if it were up to you?**

> I would spend the money on making bins impossible to ignore rather than on fines. The footprint trial in this report cut litter by a third for the price of some paint, which is remarkable, so I would paint footprints in every block and repaint them when they fade. Secondly, I would put a small bin at the entrance of every lift lobby, because that is where people finish their drinks while waiting. Thirdly, and this matters most to me, I would have primary school classes spend one morning a term clearing their own estate with the cleaners. It is easy to drop a cup when litter feels like somebody else's job, and much harder once you have spent a morning bending down to pick it up yourself. Fines punish the few people who are caught. Changing how the place feels changes everybody.

**Q1. Looking at this chart, what stands out to you?**

> The chart shows that the car is by far the most common way pupils get to school, at 41%, while only about one in five walks. What I find most surprising is the line underneath. **Q2. How do you get to school, and what do you like or dislike about it?**

> I walk, because I live in the block behind the school, and it takes me about eight minutes. What I like best is that I walk with my neighbour Siti and we talk the whole way, which means I arrive awake instead of half asleep. What I dislike is the rain. > I think a school should make walking feel organised rather than just allowed. One idea I have read about is a walking bus, where a parent walks a fixed route at a fixed time and children join along the way. That would help with the morning rush, because parents would not each have to make their own trip. Secondly, I would put up a covered walkway on the busiest path, since rain is the reason most people give up walking. Finally, I would give each class a chart of how many walking days it has managed in a month, because my class will compete over absolutely anything.

**Stimulus summary:**   PET SHOP NOTICE · BEFORE YOU BUY  Is your family ready for a pet?    Fresh food and water  Cleaning the cage  Time out of the cage  Visit to the vet    every day   twice a week   every day   once a year   A rabbit lives for  8 to 12 years . **Q1. What does this notice tell you about keeping a rabbit?**

> It tells me that a rabbit is a lot more work than I would have guessed. It needs fresh hay every single day and time outside its cage every day, and the cage has to be cleaned twice a week. The part that surprised me most is that a rabbit can live for eight to twelve years, which is almost as long as I have been alive. **Q2. Tell me about an animal you have looked after, or one you would like to look after.**

> My family looks after my neighbour's cat, Tiger, whenever they travel. It is my job to feed him in the morning and change his water. The first time, I put down too much food and my mother said he would be sick, so now I use the small cup that came with the packet. > I think the first thing to ask is who will actually do the work, because in my experience the person who wants the pet most is not always the person who ends up feeding it. The last thing I would think about is how long the animal lives. If a rabbit lives for ten years, a Primary 3 pupil getting one now will be in secondary school before it is old, and somebody has to plan for that.

**Q1. What do you notice about this survey?**

> I notice that the jobs children do most are the ones closest to themselves. > I am in charge of the rubbish. Every evening after dinner I tie the bag and take it down to the chute on our floor. The second reason is that these are things we will all need to do one day anyway, and it is much easier to learn them at nine than to learn them all at once when you are older. I would add one thing, though. I think it works better when a child is given one job to own rather than being asked to help with whatever is left, because this survey says exactly that.

### Second-hand things  ·  *P5 and above*

*(The stimulus for this topic is shown in the app under Practice. A description is given below so the conversation can also be done from this document.)*

**Stimulus summary:**   NATIONAL ENVIRONMENT AGENCY · HOUSEHOLD WASTE  What we throw away that still works    Still working  Needs a small repair  Beyond repair  Unsafe or recalled    41%   27%   22%   10%   A survey of items left at five housing estates found that  41%  of the discarded electrical goods were still in working order. The commonest reason given for replacing a working item was  wanting a newer model , not breakage.  

**Q1. What do you make of the information in this survey?**

> The figure that stands out is that 41% of the electrical goods thrown away were still working. If you add the 27% that needed only a small repair, then more than two thirds of what was discarded did not actually need to be discarded at all. What I find most telling is the reason given underneath. People were not replacing things because they had broken, but because a newer model had come out, which means this is a problem about how we think rather than a problem about how well things are made.

**Q2. Tell me about something in your home that was passed on to you or repaired.**

> My desk lamp was my cousin's before it was mine. The switch had stopped working, and my father and I opened it one Saturday and found that a wire had simply come loose from the base. It took about twenty minutes to fix. What I remember is how surprised I was that it was something that small, because when it stopped working we all assumed it was finished. I have had it for three years now and I would not swap it for a new one, partly because I know what is inside it.

**Q3. How could a school encourage pupils to repair things rather than replace them?**

> I think a school should make repairing visible, because at the moment it happens out of sight and replacing happens in front of everybody. One idea would be a repair corner at recess, once a month, where a teacher or a parent who is good with tools helps pupils mend bags, zips and water bottles. Secondly, I would ask the art and science departments to run a project where pupils take something broken apart to see how it works, because you cannot want to fix what you do not understand. Finally, and this matters most, I would stop the school rewarding new things. When everybody notices a new bag and nobody notices a mended one, no amount of encouragement will change what pupils actually do.

### Learning from mistakes  ·  *P5 and above*

*(The stimulus for this topic is shown in the app under Practice. A description is given below so the conversation can also be done from this document.)*

**Stimulus summary:**   SCHOOL COUNSELLING SERVICE · PUPIL SURVEY  What pupils do after getting something wrong    Looked at the mark only  Reread the questions alone  Went through it with somebody  Redid the paper    58%   24%   13%   5%   Of 520 pupils asked what they did after a poor test result,  58%  said they looked at the mark and put the paper away. Pupils who went through their wrong answers with somebody scored on average  nine marks higher  on the next test of the same topic.  

**Q1. What does this survey tell you?**

> It tells me that most pupils, well over half, treat a test as something that is finished once they know the mark. Only about one in eight goes through the wrong answers with another person, and yet that is the group whose next result was nine marks better. What makes the figure persuasive to me is that it compares the same topic, so it is not simply that the cleverer pupils talk about their work. It suggests the talking is what does the work.

**Q2. Tell me about a mistake of your own that you learnt something from.**

> In Primary 4 I lost nine marks in a Science paper because I answered the question I had expected rather than the one that was printed. It asked why the plant in the dark box survived for two weeks, and I wrote everything I knew about photosynthesis instead. My teacher made me read the question aloud, twice, and then asked me what the word 'survived' was doing in it. I have underlined the verb in every question since. I still lose marks, but not for that.

**Q3. Should schools give pupils time in class to go over their mistakes?**

> I strongly believe they should, for a reason this survey makes plain: pupils will not do it on their own. Only 13% went through their wrong answers with anybody, so leaving it to homework means it mostly does not happen. A lesson spent on the paper you have just sat is also more useful than a new lesson, because you already care about the answers. I would add one condition, though. It has to be done in a way that is not embarrassing. If going over mistakes means reading your score out, pupils will hide the paper, and the school will have made the problem worse rather than better.

### Homework and free time  ·  *P6 and above*

*(The stimulus for this topic is shown in the app under Practice. A description is given below so the conversation can also be done from this document.)*

**Stimulus summary:**   STRAITS RESEARCH · PRIMARY 6 TIME DIARY  Where a Primary 6 day goes    School and travel  Homework and tuition  Meals and chores  Free time, unstructured    9h 06m   3h 24m   1h 36m   54m   Pupils kept a diary for one school week. The averages below exclude sleep. Of the pupils who reported under half an hour of unstructured free time a day,  71%  also reported finding it hard to think of something to do when they did have time.  

**Q1. What is the most striking thing in this diary study?**

> The bare numbers are striking enough: nine hours on school and travel against fifty-four minutes of unstructured time. But the sentence underneath is what I keep returning to. Among the pupils with the least free time, 71% said they found it hard to think of what to do when they finally had some. I would have assumed those pupils would be the most eager to use it. It suggests that deciding what to do with yourself is a skill, and that it gets rusty if every hour of your week has already been decided for you by somebody else.

**Q2. How does your own week compare?**

> Mine is close to this, though my homework is nearer two and a half hours because I do not have tuition on Wednesdays. The free time figure feels about right, and I recognise the problem in that last sentence. On the first Saturday of the June holidays I genuinely did not know what to do with myself and ended up scrolling for two hours, which I did not enjoy. What I have since found works is having one or two things I am slowly working at — at the moment it is learning to juggle — so that free time arrives with something already waiting in it.

**Q3. Some people say children today have too little free time. What do you think?**

> I agree, but I want to be careful about why, because the usual argument is that children are tired, and tiredness is only part of it. What this study points to is more interesting: free time is where you find out what you actually like, and that cannot be scheduled for you. A child whose whole week is arranged may do very well at school and still reach secondary school without knowing what they would choose to do with an afternoon. I would not argue for less work. I would argue for protecting one genuinely empty afternoon a week and resisting the temptation to fill it, which I think is harder for parents than it sounds.

## Marking Reading Aloud at home

| Out of 10 | What it sounds like |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | Fluent throughout, clear final consonants, expression matches the meaning, well-judged pace |
| 7–8 | Mostly fluent, one or two stumbles, some expression |
| 5–6 | Several stumbles or mispronunciations, flat delivery, rushed |
| 3–4 | Frequent hesitation, words guessed, little sense of the meaning |
| 1–2 | Very halting |

## Marking the Conversation at home

| Out of 20 | What it sounds like |
|---|---|
| 17–20 | Every answer developed over several sentences with a personal example, clear opinions, natural delivery, good eye contact |
| 13–16 | Answers developed but one is short, or examples are general rather than personal |
| 9–12 | Answers of one or two sentences, opinions stated but not explained |
| 5–8 | Very short answers, long pauses, needs prompting |
| 1–4 | Barely responds |

> Score the child honestly, tell them the one thing to change, and do the same stimulus again a week later. The improvement between attempt one and attempt two is usually larger than anything a term of lessons produces.
