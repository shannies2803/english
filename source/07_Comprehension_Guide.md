# Comprehension Guide — Paper 2 Open-Ended (20 marks)

Comprehension is the largest single component of Paper 2 Booklet B, and the one where marks are most often lost to **technique** rather than understanding. Most children understand the passage perfectly well and still lose six or seven marks, because they answer the question they expected instead of the question that was asked.

This guide has two parts: the eight question types and how each one must be answered, then four full passages with model answers.

---

## Part 1 — The Eight Question Types

### 1. "Why…?" questions  ·  *1–2m*

Your answer must be a reason, and it must start in a way that answers 'why'. Begin with 'Because…' or 'This is because…'. If the question says 'Why do you think…', the reason is not stated in the passage — you must infer it and support it with something that is.

> **Question:** Why did Meilin hide the letter under her mattress?
>
> ❌ **Weak:** She put it under her mattress.
>
> ✅ **Strong:** Because she did not want her mother to find out that she had failed the test.
>
> *The weak answer repeats the question. The strong answer supplies the reason the question asked for.*

### 2. "How do you know…?" questions  ·  *1–2m*

This asks for evidence. Point to something specific in the passage — an action, a word, a detail. Do not answer with an opinion or a feeling.

> **Question:** How do you know that the old man was poor?
>
> ❌ **Weak:** Because he looked very poor and sad.
>
> ✅ **Strong:** His shoes were held together with tape and he counted out his coins before buying a single bun.
>
> *Evidence means details you can point at, not restating the conclusion.*

### 3. "How did X feel?" questions  ·  *1–2m*

Name a precise emotion, then justify it. Avoid 'sad', 'happy', 'bad' — reach for relieved, humiliated, resentful, apprehensive, remorseful, indignant. Two marks usually means one mark for the emotion and one for the reason.

> **Question:** How did Devi feel when the results were read out? (2m)
>
> ❌ **Weak:** She felt bad.
>
> ✅ **Strong:** She felt humiliated, because her name was called out last in front of the whole class.
>
> *One precise word plus a 'because' clause earns both marks.*

### 4. Vocabulary-in-context questions  ·  *1m*

Replace the word with your own, and make sure your replacement fits the sentence grammatically. Read the sentence back with your word in it. If the word is a verb in the past tense, your answer must be a verb in the past tense.

> **Question:** What does the word 'trudged' tell us about how the boy walked?
>
> ❌ **Weak:** It means he walked.
>
> ✅ **Strong:** He walked slowly and heavily, as if he was tired or unwilling.
>
> *Explain the extra meaning the writer chose that word for, not just the basic action.*

### 5. "What does 'it' / 'this' refer to?"  ·  *1m*

Look at the sentence before. Write out the thing in full — never answer with another pronoun. Check by substituting your answer back into the original sentence.

> **Question:** In line 14, what does 'this' refer to?
>
> ❌ **Weak:** It refers to what he did.
>
> ✅ **Strong:** The fact that Kumar had returned the wallet without taking any money from it.
>
> *'What he did' is still vague. Name the thing.*

### 6. Questions that say "in your own words"  ·  *2m*

You will lose the mark if you lift the phrase from the passage. Change the key words, not just the small ones. Keep the meaning identical.

> **Question:** In your own words, explain why the villagers were reluctant to leave.
>
> ❌ **Weak:** They were reluctant to leave because they had lived there all their lives.
>
> ✅ **Strong:** They did not want to go because the village had been their home since they were born.
>
> *'Reluctant' and 'all their lives' were both lifted in the weak version.*

### 7. True / False with evidence  ·  *2m*

One mark for the tick, one for the evidence. The evidence must be the specific line that proves it — not a general summary. Always fill in the evidence box even when you are unsure of the tick.

> **Question:** The narrator's father approved of her decision. True or false? Give evidence.
>
> ❌ **Weak:** False, he did not approve.
>
> ✅ **Strong:** False — 'He said nothing for a long moment, then folded the form in half and set it aside.'
>
> *The evidence must do work. Repeating the statement is not evidence.*

### 8. The 3-mark 'explain' question  ·  *3m*

Three marks means three separate points, not one point written three times. Plan them on your fingers before writing. Use a connector between each so the marker can see them: 'Firstly… In addition… Finally…'

> **Question:** Explain how the writer creates a sense of danger in paragraph 4. (3m)
>
> ❌ **Weak:** The writer makes it sound scary by describing the storm.
>
> ✅ **Strong:** Firstly, the writer uses short sentences such as 'The rope snapped.' to make events feel sudden. In addition, the sounds described — the groaning of the timber and the shriek of the wind — suggest the structure is about to fail. Finally, the narrator's own body betrays her fear: her fingers are described as numb and useless.
>
> *Three distinct points, clearly signposted.*

## How to work through a comprehension passage

1. **Read the questions first.** Two minutes spent here saves ten later — you read the passage knowing what to look for.
2. **Read the passage once, without stopping.** Do not annotate yet. Get the whole shape of it.
3. **Read again with a pencil.** Underline anything that answers a question, and write the question number in the margin beside it.
4. **Check the marks.** One mark means one point. Three marks means three separate points. Never write a paragraph for a one-mark question, and never write one sentence for a three-mark question.
5. **Answer in full sentences**, unless the question asks for a single word.
6. **Re-read your answer against the question.** The commonest error in the whole paper is answering a related question rather than the one printed.

### The lifting trap

Lifting means copying a phrase straight from the passage. It is allowed for some questions and fatal for others:

| Question says | Lifting |
|---|---|
| "Give evidence" / "Quote" | ✅ Expected — copy exactly |
| Nothing about wording | ⚠️ Allowed, but adapt so it reads as an answer |
| "In your own words" | ❌ Zero marks if you lift the key words |

## The Kite

*Narrative · about 330 words · 8 questions · 14 marks · suggested for P4 and above*

Ah Kong made the kite himself, on the floor of the corridor outside our flat. He split the bamboo with a knife so old the handle had worn smooth, and he bent each strip over a candle flame until it curved exactly the way he wanted. It took him four evenings. I sat beside him the whole time, passing him string he did not ask for.

"Why don't we just buy one?" I asked on the third evening. There was a shop at the void deck selling kites shaped like eagles, with printed feathers and plastic tails.

Ah Kong did not look up. "You can buy a kite," he said. "You cannot buy a kite that knows you."

I did not understand that, but I nodded anyway.

On Sunday we walked to the open field behind the school. The wind was strong and the sky was the pale, washed-out blue that comes after heavy rain. Ah Kong held the kite above his head and told me to run. I ran until my chest burned, and when I turned around the kite was up, small and steady, tugging at the line like something alive.

For nearly an hour it stayed there. Then the wind changed. The line went slack, then snapped taut, and before I could loosen my grip the string broke somewhere far above my hands. The kite tumbled sideways across the sky and disappeared behind the trees at the edge of the field.

I stood there with the empty spool in my fist. Four evenings of bamboo and candle flame, gone in three seconds. My eyes stung and I turned away so Ah Kong would not see.

He came and stood beside me, looking at the trees.

"Good," he said.

I stared at him.

"Now," he said, "you know how to make one."

We walked home. That evening he took out the knife and put a fresh strip of bamboo in my hands, and this time he did not touch it at all. He only watched, and told me when to move the flame closer.

### Questions

**1.** How long did Ah Kong take to make the first kite? *(1m)*

**2.** Why did the narrator suggest buying a kite instead? (1m)

**3.** What did Ah Kong mean when he said, "You cannot buy a kite that knows you"? (2m)

**4.** In your own words, describe the weather on Sunday. (2m)

**5.** What does the phrase "tugging at the line like something alive" suggest about the kite? *(1m)*

**6.** How did the narrator feel when the kite was lost? How do you know? (2m)

**7.** Why do you think Ah Kong said "Good" when the kite was lost? (2m)

**8.** How is the last paragraph different from the beginning of the story? Explain what this shows. (3m)

### Model answers

**1.** Four evenings.  `[1m]`

> A literal question — the answer is stated directly. Don't over-write it.

**2.** Because there was a shop at the void deck selling ready-made kites shaped like eagles, so it would have been much quicker and easier than making one.  `[1m]`

> Start with 'Because'. The reason is implied by the detail about the shop.

**3.** He meant that a kite you have made yourself has value that a bought one does not, because you have put your own time and effort into it and understand how every part of it works.  `[2m]`

> Inference. The answer is nowhere in the passage — you build it from what he does afterwards.

**4.** It was breezy, and the sky was a faint, faded blue because a heavy shower had fallen earlier.  `[2m]`

> 'Pale, washed-out blue' and 'after heavy rain' must both be reworded, not copied.

**5.** It suggests that the kite pulled strongly and unpredictably, as though it had a will of its own rather than being an ordinary object.  `[1m]`

> Vocabulary-in-context: explain the extra meaning the comparison creates.

**6.** He felt devastated and close to tears. I know this because his eyes stung and he deliberately turned away so that Ah Kong would not see him crying.  `[2m]`

> One mark for a precise emotion, one for the evidence. 'Sad' alone would be weak.

**7.** Because he was not upset about losing the kite itself. He saw that his grandson had now watched the whole process closely enough to build one himself, which was what he had wanted all along.  `[2m]`

> 'Why do you think' signals inference. Support it with the final paragraph.

**8.** Firstly, at the beginning Ah Kong does all the work while the narrator only sits and passes string. In addition, at the end it is the narrator who holds the bamboo and the knife, while Ah Kong does not touch it at all. Finally, this shows that the narrator has moved from being a bystander to being the maker, which is the lesson Ah Kong intended.  `[3m]`

> Three marks means three separate points. Signpost them.

## Mrs Fernandez

*Narrative · about 350 words · 8 questions · 16 marks · suggested for P5 and above*

We decided we hated her before she had said a word.

She came in on the Monday after Mrs Koh went on maternity leave — a small, grey-haired woman in a cardigan the colour of weak tea, carrying a canvas bag that looked heavier than she was. She set the bag down, wrote MRS FERNANDEZ on the board in careful capitals, and then stood looking at us for what felt like a very long time.

"She's ancient," Priya whispered. "She'll be hopeless."

For the first fortnight, she was. She spoke too softly. She did not notice when Wei Jie flicked erasers across the room. When she asked a question and nobody answered, she simply waited, sometimes for half a minute, until the silence became unbearable and somebody muttered something just to end it. We complained loudly to one another at recess. Someone said she should retire.

Then, in the third week, she handed back our compositions.

Mine came back covered in small, neat pencil marks. Not crosses — questions. Beside a sentence where I had written that my character was angry, she had written: *What did his hands do?* Beside my ending, where I had explained the moral in two tidy lines, she had written: *You already showed me this. Trust me to see it.*

Nobody had ever written to me in the margin of my own work before. Teachers wrote grades and 'good effort'. Mrs Fernandez had read the thing.

I looked around. Priya was frowning at her own page, turning it over, reading the back. Wei Jie, who had not written more than half a page all year, was reading his in complete silence.

"Rewrite them if you want to," Mrs Fernandez said. "I'll read them again. I'll read them as many times as you like."

Twenty-six of us rewrote them. Some of us rewrote them twice.

She was with us for eleven weeks. I cannot remember a single thing she said in front of the class. I remember every word she wrote in my margins.

### Questions

**1.** Why was Mrs Fernandez teaching the class? (1m)

**2.** Give two reasons why the class disliked Mrs Fernandez at first. (2m)

**3.** What does the phrase "a cardigan the colour of weak tea" suggest about the impression Mrs Fernandez made? (2m)

**4.** In your own words, explain what was unusual about the marks on the narrator's composition. (2m)

**5.** What did Mrs Fernandez mean by "You already showed me this. Trust me to see it."? (2m)

**6.** Why do you think the writer mentions that Wei Jie read his composition "in complete silence"? (2m)

**7.** "The narrator's opinion of Mrs Fernandez changed gradually." True or false? Give evidence. (2m)

**8.** Explain why the last two sentences are an effective ending to the story. (3m)

### Model answers

**1.** Because their usual teacher, Mrs Koh, had gone on maternity leave.  `[1m]`

> Literal. One line is enough.

**2.** Firstly, she spoke too quietly for them to hear her easily. Secondly, she failed to control the class — she did not notice Wei Jie flicking erasers around the room.  `[2m]`

> Two marks, two distinct reasons. Long silences would also be accepted.

**3.** It suggests she looked faded and dull, and that nothing about her appearance was striking or impressive — which is exactly why the class dismissed her so quickly.  `[2m]`

> Vocabulary in context. Explain the impression, not just the colour.

**4.** Instead of correcting errors or giving a grade, she had written questions in the margin that pushed him to think about his own writing choices.  `[2m]`

> 'Not crosses — questions' must be reworded, not lifted.

**5.** She meant that the narrator's story had already made its lesson clear through the events themselves, so spelling out the moral at the end was unnecessary and he should have confidence in his reader.  `[2m]`

> Inference from a quoted line. Link it back to the composition advice about not announcing the moral.

**6.** Because Wei Jie had been the most disruptive pupil and had barely written anything all year. His silence shows that even he had been affected by her comments, which proves how unusual her marking was.  `[2m]`

> The detail is chosen deliberately. Ask why the writer picked that particular boy.

**7.** True. The class hated her from the first day and still complained at recess for a fortnight, but it was only in the third week, after she returned their compositions, that his view began to change.  `[2m]`

> One mark for true/false, one for the specific evidence.

**8.** Firstly, they contrast what the narrator cannot remember with what he can, which makes the point sharply. In addition, they show that Mrs Fernandez's real influence was private and written rather than loud and public, overturning the class's early judgement that she was hopeless. Finally, the words 'every word' suggest her comments stayed with him long after she left, which tells us how much she mattered.  `[3m]`

> Three marks, three points, signposted.

## What We Throw Away

*Non-narrative · about 340 words · 8 questions · 15 marks · suggested for P5 and above*

Every year, Singapore throws away enough food to fill more than 700 Olympic swimming pools. Around half of this waste comes not from restaurants or supermarkets, but from ordinary households — from kitchens like yours and mine.

The reasons are surprisingly ordinary. Shoppers buy in bulk because larger packets appear cheaper, then find they cannot finish the food before it spoils. Others cook far more than their family can eat, because serving too little is considered impolite. And a great deal of perfectly good food is discarded simply because of the date printed on the packet.

That date is widely misunderstood. A 'use by' date is a safety instruction: after it, the food may genuinely be unsafe. A 'best before' date is only a guide to quality. A packet of biscuits a week past its 'best before' date may be slightly less crisp, but it is not dangerous. Research suggests that confusion between these two labels alone accounts for a significant share of household food waste.

The consequences reach further than most people realise. Food that is thrown away must be incinerated, and the ash is shipped to Pulau Semakau, Singapore's only landfill. Semakau is expected to run out of space within a few decades. Beyond that, every discarded meal represents wasted water, wasted fuel and wasted farmland, most of it in other countries, since Singapore imports over ninety per cent of what it eats.

The solutions, fortunately, are also ordinary. Planning meals before shopping cuts impulse buying. Storing food correctly — vegetables in the right part of the fridge, rice in a sealed container — extends its life considerably. Cooking smaller portions and returning for a second helping wastes far less than filling every plate to the brim. Several community fridges now allow residents to leave surplus food for neighbours instead of binning it.

None of these actions is dramatic. But food waste is not a problem created by a few careless people. It is created by millions of small, reasonable decisions — which means it can be undone the same way.

### Questions

**1.** Where does about half of Singapore's food waste come from? (1m)

**2.** Give two reasons, in your own words, why households buy or cook too much food. (2m)

**3.** Explain the difference between a 'use by' date and a 'best before' date. (2m)

**4.** What does the word 'discarded' mean in paragraph 2? *(1m)*

**5.** Why is food waste a particular problem for Singapore? Give two reasons. (2m)

**6.** Suggest two things a family could do this week to waste less food. (2m)

**7.** What does the writer mean by "None of these actions is dramatic"? (2m)

**8.** Explain how the final sentence sums up the writer's argument. (3m)

### Model answers

**1.** From ordinary households, rather than from restaurants or supermarkets.  `[1m]`

> Literal retrieval from paragraph 1.

**2.** Firstly, bigger packets look like better value, so people purchase more than they can finish before it goes bad. Secondly, offering guests or family too small a serving is seen as rude, so people prepare extra.  `[2m]`

> Both reasons must be reworded. 'Impolite' and 'in bulk' should not appear.

**3.** A 'use by' date warns that the food could become unsafe to eat after that day, whereas a 'best before' date only indicates when the food stops being at its best in taste or texture, though it remains safe.  `[2m]`

> Two marks means both halves of the contrast must appear.

**4.** Thrown away or got rid of.  `[1m]`

> Give a synonym that fits grammatically — 'discarded' is a past participle, so 'thrown away' works.

**5.** Firstly, the ash from incinerated waste goes to Pulau Semakau, the country's only landfill, which is expected to be full within a few decades. Secondly, Singapore imports more than ninety per cent of its food, so wasted meals also waste the water, fuel and farmland used to produce them overseas.  `[2m]`

> Two distinct reasons, both from paragraph 4.

**6.** They could plan their meals before going shopping so that they only buy what they will actually use, and serve smaller portions at meals, allowing anyone still hungry to take a second helping.  `[2m]`

> An application question. Answers must come from or be consistent with the passage.

**7.** The writer means that the solutions are all small, everyday changes rather than difficult or impressive measures, so anyone can carry them out without much effort.  `[2m]`

> Explain the phrase, then say why the writer includes it.

**8.** Firstly, it rejects the idea that food waste is caused by a small number of careless individuals. In addition, it explains that the problem is the result of countless small, sensible decisions made by ordinary people. Finally, it argues that because the cause is small and shared, the remedy can be small and shared too, which gives the reader reason to believe their own actions matter.  `[3m]`

> Three points. The last one is the persuasive purpose of the sentence.

## The Last Bus

*Narrative · about 360 words · 8 questions · 16 marks · suggested for P6 and above*

The plan had been simple, which is how I know now that it was mine.

Ravi was nine and slower than me at everything, so I told him to wait at the bench outside the library while I went to buy the birthday card. Ten minutes. Fifteen at most. He had his water bottle and the book he was halfway through, and he nodded at me the way he always did, as though whatever I said had already been decided by someone more important than either of us.

The stationery shop had a queue. Then they did not have the card I wanted, so I walked to the second shop, which was two streets away, and then I saw Marcus and Hui Ling outside the bubble tea place and we talked about the school camp.

When I looked at my phone it was 6.42 p.m.

I ran. I remember the sound of my own bag hitting my back, over and over, and the way the shophouses blurred at the edges. The library steps were empty. The bench was empty. There was a water bottle underneath it, on its side.

I called his name once, loudly, and it came back off the wall of the building and sounded like somebody else's voice.

For four minutes I stood in the middle of the pavement and did nothing useful at all. I thought about the canal behind the library. I thought about what I would say to my mother. I thought, with a clarity that shames me still, about how none of this would have happened if he were faster.

Then a bus pulled in at the stop across the road — the 168, the one we took home — and Ravi got off it.

He had his book under his arm. He looked at my face and something in his own face changed, and he said, before I could speak, "I'm sorry. I thought you'd gone home. I thought I'd got it wrong."

He apologised to me. I have thought about that a great deal since.

I am seventeen now. Ravi is taller than me and beats me at everything. But I still check, every single time we leave somewhere, that he is behind me. He has noticed. He has never once mentioned it.

### Questions

**1.** What was the narrator's plan? (1m)

**2.** Give two reasons why the narrator took much longer than planned. (2m)

**3.** What does the opening sentence, "The plan had been simple, which is how I know now that it was mine", suggest about the narrator? (2m)

**4.** Why does the writer mention the water bottle lying on its side? (2m)

**5.** In your own words, describe what the narrator did during the four minutes after she called out. (2m)

**6.** The narrator says one of her thoughts "shames me still". Which thought, and why is she ashamed of it? (2m)

**7.** Why do you think Ravi apologised to the narrator? (2m)

**8.** Explain how the final paragraph shows that the narrator was changed by what happened. (3m)

### Model answers

**1.** To leave Ravi waiting on the bench outside the library for ten to fifteen minutes while she went to buy a birthday card.  `[1m]`

> Literal, but include the time — it matters later.

**2.** Firstly, the first stationery shop had a queue and did not stock the card she wanted, so she walked two streets to a second shop. Secondly, she then met Marcus and Hui Ling and stopped to chat about the school camp.  `[2m]`

> Two separate delays. The second one is the one she is ashamed of.

**3.** It suggests that she is looking back as an older person and judging her younger self, recognising that the plan seemed simple only because she had not thought about what could go wrong.  `[2m]`

> Note the phrase 'know now' — the story is told in hindsight.

**4.** Because it is evidence that Ravi had been there and has gone, and the fact that it is knocked over suggests he may have left in a hurry, which increases the narrator's alarm.  `[2m]`

> Small physical details in narratives are almost always doing a job. Say what job.

**5.** She stood still on the pavement, unable to take any helpful action, while frightening thoughts ran through her mind.  `[2m]`

> 'Did nothing useful at all' must be reworded.

**6.** The thought that none of it would have happened if Ravi had been faster. She is ashamed because in a moment when her brother might have been in danger, she was blaming him for a situation she herself had caused.  `[2m]`

> Identify the thought, then explain the shame. Two clear parts.

**7.** Because he assumed he had made the mistake himself by misunderstanding her instructions, and because he saw how upset she looked and wanted to make things better — he was used to accepting that she was in charge.  `[2m]`

> Link back to paragraph 2, where he defers to her.

**8.** Firstly, although many years have passed and Ravi is now the more capable of the two, she still checks that he is behind her every time they leave a place. In addition, the phrase 'every single time' shows this has become a permanent habit rather than an occasional worry. Finally, the detail that Ravi has noticed but never mentioned it suggests he understands why she does it, and that the incident quietly shaped both of them.  `[3m]`

> Three marks, three points. The final one is about Ravi, not just the narrator.

## The Spare Room

*Narrative · about 320 words · 8 questions · 16 marks · suggested for P4 and above*

When my grandmother came to live with us, she was given the spare room, and the spare room was where I had kept everything that mattered to me.

It was not really a bedroom. It had a folding bed nobody used, three boxes of my father's old files, and — most importantly — a corner where I had built a city out of cardboard over two entire school holidays. It had a hospital. It had a river made of blue tissue paper. I had spent forty-one days on it, because I had counted.

My mother told me on a Tuesday. Grandmother was moving in on Saturday, and the city would have to go.

I did not argue, because in our house arguing about a decision that has already been made is considered a waste of everybody's afternoon. I took the city apart myself on Friday evening, piece by piece, and put it in the recycling bin downstairs. It took eleven minutes. I remember being surprised by that.

For the first two weeks, I did not go into the spare room at all.

Then one evening Grandmother called me in. She was sitting on the edge of the bed with a shoebox on her lap, and inside the shoebox were about thirty small objects wrapped in newspaper — a brass thimble, a wooden elephant with one ear missing, a key to a house in Ipoh that had been sold in 1978.

She unwrapped them one at a time and told me where each had come from. It took nearly an hour, and she did not hurry any of it.

At the end she said, "Your mother tells me there was a city here."

I said there had been.

She thought about that. Then she said, "Mine is in a shoebox. Yours was on the floor. Neither is very sensible."

I laughed before I could stop myself.

We built a new one together over the December holidays, on a piece of board that slides under her bed. It is smaller than the first. It has a hospital, a blue tissue river, and, at Grandmother's insistence, a shop that sells nothing but thimbles.

### Questions

**1.** What had the narrator built in the spare room? (1m)

**2.** How long had the city taken to build, and how do we know the narrator cared about it? (2m)

**3.** Why did the narrator not argue with his mother? (2m)

**4.** Why do you think the narrator was surprised that it only took eleven minutes? (2m)

**5.** Describe two of the objects in Grandmother's shoebox. (1m)

**6.** What did Grandmother mean by "Mine is in a shoebox. Yours was on the floor. Neither is very sensible."? (3m)

**7.** How did the narrator's feelings towards his grandmother change? Use evidence. (3m)

**8.** Why is the detail about the thimble shop an effective ending? (2m)

### Model answers

**1.** A city made out of cardboard, complete with a hospital and a river made from blue tissue paper.  `[1m]`

> Literal, but include a detail or two.

**2.** It took forty-one days across two school holidays. We know he cared because he had counted the days exactly.  `[2m]`

> Two parts to the question — answer both.

**3.** Because in his family, arguing about something that has already been decided is regarded as pointless, so there was no expectation that complaining would change anything.  `[2m]`

> The reason is stated, but must be reworded a little.

**4.** Because something that had taken him forty-one days to build was destroyed in a few minutes, and the difference between the two made the loss feel sharper and somehow unfair.  `[2m]`

> 'Why do you think' — inference. Contrast the two numbers.

**5.** A brass thimble and a wooden elephant missing one ear.  `[1m]`

> One mark, so keep it very short.

**6.** Firstly, she meant that she too keeps things that matter to her, so she understood his loss. In addition, she was pointing out that neither of them stores their treasures in a practical way. Finally, by joking about them both she put herself on his side rather than treating him as a child who had lost a toy.  `[3m]`

> Three marks means three points. The third one is about her purpose in saying it.

**7.** Firstly, at the start he resented her arrival, shown by his avoiding the spare room completely for a fortnight. In addition, after she showed him the shoebox and made her remark, he laughed before he could stop himself, which suggests his resistance broke. Finally, by December they were building a new city together, which shows the relationship had become a genuine friendship.  `[3m]`

> Track the change across the whole passage: before, turning point, after.

**8.** Because it shows the new city belongs to both of them rather than only to the narrator, and the small joke about her own treasured object proves that she has been fully included in something that once mattered only to him.  `[2m]`

> Endings that use a small specific detail almost always carry a larger meaning.

## Why We Sleep Badly

*Non-narrative · about 340 words · 8 questions · 16 marks · suggested for P5 and above*

Ask a class of Primary Six pupils how many hours they slept last night and the answers will vary widely. Ask how many hours they should have slept, and most will guess correctly: nine to eleven. The gap between what children know and what they do is one of the more puzzling features of childhood sleep.

Part of the explanation is biological. During adolescence, the body clock shifts later, so teenagers genuinely find it harder to fall asleep early. This shift begins earlier than most parents realise, sometimes as young as eleven. A child who says she is not tired at half past nine may be telling the truth.

A larger part of the explanation, however, is environmental. Screens emit blue light, which suppresses melatonin, the hormone that signals to the body that it is time to sleep. Watching a video for thirty minutes before bed can delay the onset of sleep by considerably longer than thirty minutes. The problem is not simply that screens take up time; it is that they change what the body does with the time that remains.

There is also the matter of what researchers call sleep debt. Many children lose an hour or two on school nights and attempt to repay it by sleeping late at weekends. This does not work as well as it feels like it should. Sleeping until noon on Sunday shifts the body clock later again, which makes Monday morning harder than it needed to be — a cycle sometimes called social jet lag.

The consequences are not limited to feeling tired. Sleep is when the brain consolidates what was learnt during the day, moving information from short-term into long-term memory. A pupil who revises for two hours and then sleeps for five may retain less than one who revises for one hour and sleeps for nine.

The remedies are unglamorous. A consistent bedtime, including at weekends. No screens for an hour before sleep. A dark, cool room. None of this is new advice, and none of it is difficult. It is simply, for most families, inconvenient.

## Why Cities Are Getting Hotter

*Non-narrative · about 345 words · 8 questions · 15 marks · suggested for P5 and above*

On a still afternoon, the centre of a large city can be as much as seven degrees warmer than the countryside a few kilometres away. Scientists call this the urban heat island effect, and Singapore experiences one of the strongest examples in the world.

The causes are mostly matters of material. Concrete, asphalt and brick absorb heat during the day and release it slowly through the night, which is why city nights cool down far less than rural ones. A dark road surface can reach sixty degrees in the afternoon sun. Grass in the same conditions stays close to air temperature, because plants release water vapour through their leaves, and evaporation cools the air around them.

Shape matters as well as material. Tall buildings standing close together trap heat between them in what researchers call an urban canyon. Heat that would otherwise radiate upwards into the sky bounces instead from wall to wall. The same buildings block the wind that would carry warm air away.

Then there is the heat we generate ourselves. Every air conditioner cools a room by moving heat outdoors. Every vehicle engine, every kitchen, every server room adds warmth to the street. On the hottest days, when air conditioners are working hardest, this contribution is at its greatest — which means the problem worsens precisely when it is least welcome.

The consequences are not merely uncomfortable. Higher night temperatures disturb sleep, and poor sleep affects health and concentration. Elderly residents and those without air conditioning are most at risk. Energy use rises, which in turn generates more heat.

There are remedies, though none is a complete answer. Planting trees along streets provides shade and evaporative cooling at the same time. Roofs painted white or planted with vegetation absorb far less heat than dark ones. Designing new estates so that buildings channel rather than block the prevailing wind can lower street temperatures measurably.

None of these measures will make a tropical city cool. What they can do is prevent it from becoming several degrees hotter than it needs to be, which over a lifetime is not a small difference.

## Why Some Animals Play Dead

*Non-narrative · about 300 words · 10 questions · 18 marks · suggested for P4 and above*

When an opossum is threatened, it does not run away. It falls over, opens its mouth, lets its tongue hang out and stops moving. It can stay like this for up to four hours.

This behaviour is often called playing dead, but the name is misleading. The animal is not pretending. Scientists have found that the opossum's heart rate drops sharply and that it becomes genuinely unable to move. It is closer to fainting than to acting.

The strategy works because of how predators hunt. Many hunting animals are triggered by movement, and a great many will not eat something they did not kill themselves, because an animal that is already dead may be diseased. A motionless opossum is therefore both harder to notice and less appetising.

Opossums are not alone. Certain snakes roll onto their backs. Some beetles fold their legs and drop from leaves. A few species of frog do it. So do some fish. The behaviour has appeared separately in many unrelated animals, which suggests it works well enough to be worth evolving more than once.

It is not without cost. An animal lying still cannot feed, cannot escape a second attacker and cannot protect its young. Some opossums have been run over because they froze in the road rather than moving. The strategy only makes sense against a predator that hunts by sight and movement, and it is useless against one that hunts by smell.

There is also a limit to how long it can last. An opossum in this state slowly recovers whether or not the danger has passed, and a young opossum recovers faster than an adult.

Like most things in nature, then, it is not a perfect defence. It is simply one that works often enough.

## The Case Against Bottled Water

*Non-narrative · about 385 words · 10 questions · 20 marks · suggested for P5 and above*

Singapore has some of the cleanest tap water in the world. It is tested continuously, it meets international drinking standards, and it costs roughly one-thousandth of the price of the same volume sold in a plastic bottle. And yet bottled water sales continue to rise.

The usual explanation is convenience, and there is truth in that. A bottle can be bought anywhere and carried away. But convenience does not explain the whole picture, because a refillable bottle is also portable and there are now water coolers in most malls, MRT stations and schools.

## Why We Queue

*Non-narrative · about 399 words · 9 questions · 19 marks · suggested for P5 and above*

Queuing is not natural. It has to be learnt, and different places learn it differently.

In Singapore, a queue at a bus stop forms almost without discussion. People arrive, look, and place themselves at the end. Nobody appoints anybody. There is no line painted on the ground, and no official watching.

What holds it together is not a rule but an agreement, and agreements are fragile in a particular way: they work completely until they do not work at all.

Researchers who study queues have found that a single person stepping in near the front rarely causes an argument. Instead, something quieter happens. The people behind exchange glances. Bags are shifted. Somebody clears their throat. Very often the person who pushed in retreats without a single word being said to them.

This is remarkably efficient. A rule needs an inspector, a penalty and somebody to collect it. Social disapproval needs nothing at all, and it arrives instantly.

It is also fragile. In the same studies, once two or three people pushed in and nothing happened, the queue collapsed within a minute. Those who had been waiting patiently stopped waiting, because the cost of being patient had suddenly become obvious and the benefit had disappeared.

Not every culture queues the same way. In some places a crowd at a counter is the norm, and what looks like disorder to a visitor is a system with its own rules about who is served next. Neither arrangement is naturally better. What matters is that everybody in the room has learnt the same one, which is why the most difficult queues in the world are at international airports.

This is why queue systems in busy places increasingly use numbered tickets. A ticket does not make people more honest. It makes honesty unnecessary, which is a different thing and, in a crowded clinic on a Monday morning, a more reliable one.

There is one exception worth noticing. People are far more willing to let somebody go ahead of them when a reason is given, even a thin one. In one well-known experiment, asking to skip a photocopier queue succeeded far more often when the request ended with the word 'because', regardless of what followed it.

That finding is usually presented as evidence that people are easily fooled. It might equally show something better: that most of us would rather be reasonable than be first.

"You were the only one who had already been to every rehearsal."

### Questions

**1.** Explain what an understudy is, using the passage. *(1m)*

**2.** Why did the narrator's position mean 'almost nothing at all'? *(2m)*

**3.** What did Mrs Fong tell the narrator in September, and why is her exact wording important? *(3m)*

**4.** Give two things the narrator learnt by watching rather than performing. *(2m)*

**5.** Why was the narrator able to notice these things? *(2m)*

**6.** What happened on the night of the performance? *(1m)*

**7.** Why does the narrator say he would have been only 'adequate'? *(2m)*

**8.** Which word in the passage means 'good enough, but no better'? *(1m)*

**9.** Why is Mrs Fong's last remark a good ending to the story? *(3m)*

### Model answers

**1.** Somebody who learns every line of a part and performs it only if the person who has the part cannot.  `[1m]`

> The passage explains it when Hafiz is introduced.

**2.** He was the understudy's understudy, so two people would both have had to be unable to perform before he would go on.  `[2m]`

> The reason is arithmetical.

**3.** She said he would not perform, because it would take two people being ill, which had happened once in twenty-two years. The wording matters because she said it was nearly impossible rather than impossible, and the narrator notices that difference and learns the part anyway.  `[3m]`

> Give what she said and then explain what the narrator makes of it.

**4.** Any two of: he understood what Mrs Fong was listening for when she made Pei Shan repeat a line; he saw how Hafiz fixed a difficult entrance by changing where he stood two lines earlier.  `[2m]`

> Two examples are given in the same paragraph.

**5.** Because he was never on stage he had nothing else to do but watch, so he had time to see things nobody explained to him.  `[2m]`

> The reason is about what he was not doing.

**6.** Neither Pei Shan nor Hafiz was ill, so the narrator did not perform. He watched from the third row with his parents.  `[1m]`

> The outcome is plainly stated.

**7.** Because he had spent eleven weeks watching two people who were better than he was, so he could judge his own level honestly.  `[2m]`

> He gives the reason in the same sentence.

**8.** 'Adequate'.  `[1m]`

> It is used about himself, not about the others.

**9.** It shows that the eleven weeks were not wasted after all. The thing that got him a speaking part was exactly the thing that had seemed pointless: turning up to every rehearsal when he had no chance of performing. It rewards the narrator without pretending he was talented, which is more honest and more satisfying.  `[3m]`

> Connect her remark back to the weeks that seemed to be for nothing.
