# Composition Guide — Continuous Writing (Paper 1 Part 2)

**40 marks: Content 20 + Language 20. Recommended time: 50 minutes. Minimum 150 words.**

---

## PART 1 — Understanding the Question

The PSLE Continuous Writing question gives you:
- **A theme** (one or two words, e.g. *Honesty*, *A Difficult Decision*, *Helping Someone*)
- **Three pictures** related to the theme
- The instruction that you may use **one, two, three, or none** of the pictures, but your story **must be relevant to the theme**

### The single most important rule

> **The theme is compulsory. The pictures are optional.**

Most content marks are lost because the story drifts away from the theme, not because the writing is weak. Before you write a single word, finish this sentence in your head:

> *"By the end of my story, the reader will clearly see that this story is about **________**."*

If you cannot finish that sentence, do not start writing.

### Using the pictures well

| Approach | Risk | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Use all three | Story becomes rushed and shallow | Avoid unless they connect naturally |
| Use one or two | Clean, focused plot | ✅ **Best choice** |
| Use none | Allowed, but examiners look extra hard at theme relevance | Only if you have a strong, clearly on-theme story |

**Do not describe the picture.** Use it as an object or event *inside* your story. If the picture shows a wallet, your story is not "a wallet"; it is *what happened when I found a wallet on the way home*.

---

## PART 2 — The Marking Criteria in Plain Words

### Content (20 marks) — the examiner asks:
1. Is the story **clearly relevant to the theme**?
2. Is the plot **believable and complete** (a real beginning, middle and end)?
3. Are the **details developed**, especially at the climax?
4. Is there **insight** — do we understand what the character felt and learnt?

### Language (20 marks) — the examiner asks:
1. Is the **grammar** accurate (tense, subject–verb agreement, sentence structure)?
2. Is the **vocabulary** varied and used correctly?
3. Is there **sentence variety** (not all short, not all long)?
4. Is the **paragraphing** logical?
5. Is the **spelling and punctuation** accurate?

> **The trade secret:** a simple plot told with excellent language and one well-developed climax scores far higher than an ambitious, complicated plot told badly. Do not write about kidnappings, murders or superheroes. Write about one small, real event and go deep.

## PART 3 — Planning (5–7 minutes, always)

Never skip this. Unplanned compositions run out of steam in paragraph 3.

### The 6-Box Plan

Draw six boxes on your question paper and fill them with **notes, not sentences**:

| Box | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| **1. Theme** | One sentence: what the story proves |
| **2. Setting** | Where and when |
| **3. Characters** | Who (keep it to 2–3 people, use real-sounding names) |
| **4. Problem** | What goes wrong |
| **5. Climax** | The single most tense moment — this gets the most words |
| **6. Resolution + Lesson** | How it ends and what was learnt |

### Worked example

> **Theme given:** *Perseverance*
>
> 1. **Theme:** Giving up is easy; trying again is what counts.
> 2. **Setting:** School field, Sports Day, late afternoon
> 3. **Characters:** Me (Rachel), Coach Lim, my rival Priya
> 4. **Problem:** I sprained my ankle two weeks before the 800 m final
> 5. **Climax:** The last lap — ankle throbbing, Priya pulling ahead, deciding whether to stop
> 6. **Resolution:** I finish third, not first — but I finish. Coach Lim says finishing mattered more.

Notice the resolution: **she does not win**. Realistic endings score better than fairy-tale ones.

## PART 4 — Structure: The Five-Paragraph Shape

| Para | Purpose | Approx. words |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Opening** | Hook the reader; establish setting and mood | 40–60 |
| **2. Rising action** | Introduce the problem; build tension | 60–80 |
| **3. Climax** ⭐ | The critical moment — slow it down, expand it | **90–120** |
| **4. Falling action** | Immediate aftermath | 50–70 |
| **5. Resolution & reflection** | Outcome + what was learnt | 50–70 |

**Target: 280–380 words for P6.** The minimum is 150, but 150-word compositions almost never develop the climax enough for high content marks. For P4–P5, aim for 180–280.

### The Climax Rule ⭐

The climax should take up **roughly a third of your composition**. Beginners rush it in two sentences; strong writers stretch it.

**How to stretch a climax — the slow-motion technique:**

1. **Physical sensation** — what the body feels
2. **A single sensory detail** — one sound, one smell
3. **A thought** — what is going through the character's head
4. **Dialogue or a shout** — a line of speech
5. **The action itself** — short, punchy sentences
6. **The immediate result**

**Rushed version (weak):**
> *I ran to the road and pulled the boy back. A car sped past. He was safe.*

**Expanded version (strong):**
> *My legs moved before my brain caught up. The screech of tyres tore through the afternoon air, sharp and terrible. Three metres. Two. I lunged forward, my fingers closing around the collar of the boy's T-shirt, and yanked with everything I had. We crashed onto the pavement in a tangle of limbs. A rush of hot air swept over us as the lorry thundered past, so close that I felt the grit sting my cheek. For a moment neither of us moved. Then the boy began to wail, and I realised my own hands were shaking uncontrollably.*

Same event. One is 20 words; the other is 100. The second one earns the content mark.

## PART 5 — Openings

**Never** open with: *One fine day…* / *It was a sunny Saturday morning…* / *Hi, my name is…* / *I will never forget the day when…* (overused)

### Six opening types that work

**① Action opening** — start in the middle of something happening
> *The glass shattered before I could even shout a warning.*

**② Dialogue opening** — a line of speech
> *"You promised you wouldn't tell anyone," Wei Jie whispered, his voice cracking.*

**③ Sound opening**
> *A single, piercing scream cut through the stillness of the void deck.*

**④ Feeling / thought opening**
> *I had told exactly one lie in my life, and it was about to catch up with me.*

**⑤ Setting-with-mood opening** — description that carries an emotion
> *The classroom felt unusually cold that morning, as though the walls themselves knew something was wrong.*

**⑥ Flashback opening**
> *Even now, three years later, I cannot walk past that bakery without my stomach tightening.*

> **Tip:** whichever opening you choose, your **first sentence should be short**. Short openings pull the reader in; long ones lose them.

---

## PART 6 — Endings

A good ending does two things: it **closes the event** and it **shows what changed inside the character**.

### Four endings that work

**① Reflection**
> *I have kept that crumpled ten-dollar note in my drawer ever since. It is worth nothing now, but it reminds me of the day I nearly became someone I would not have liked.*

**② Changed behaviour**
> *These days, I check on Mrs Rahim every Saturday. She always pretends she does not need the help, and I always pretend I have nothing better to do.*

**③ Full-circle** — echo the opening image
> *(Opening: the classroom felt cold.)* → *The classroom was still cold when the bell rang, but somehow I no longer minded.*

**④ Dialogue ending**
> *"You came back," she said quietly. I nodded. "I always would have."*

### Endings to avoid

❌ *…and then I woke up. It was all a dream.*
❌ *…we all lived happily ever after.*
❌ *…it was the best day of my life.* (too vague)
❌ A moral tacked on that has nothing to do with the story: *…this shows that we must always be filial.*

> The lesson should be **shown through the story**, then briefly named — never announced out of nowhere.

## PART 7 — Paragraphing and Sentence Variety

### Start a new paragraph when:
- Time changes
- Place changes
- A **new speaker** speaks
- The focus shifts to a new event or idea

### Vary your sentence openings

If four sentences in a row start with *I* or *The*, the language mark drops. Rotate through these:

| Opening type | Example |
|---|---|
| Adverb | **Suddenly,** the lights went out. |
| Prepositional phrase | **In the corner of the room,** something moved. |
| -ing phrase | **Gripping the railing tightly,** I peered over the edge. |
| -ed phrase | **Startled by the noise,** the cat bolted. |
| Subordinate clause | **Before I could react,** he had already left. |
| Dialogue | **"Wait!"** I called after her. |
| Short dramatic sentence | **Silence.** |

### Vary sentence length

A short sentence after two long ones creates impact:
> *I had spent three weeks preparing for the audition, rehearsing every night until my parents begged me to stop, and I had been certain — absolutely certain — that I was ready. **Then I forgot the first line.***

---

## PART 8 — Writing Dialogue

Dialogue makes a composition come alive, but only if it is **purposeful**. Two to four exchanges per composition is enough.

**Rules recap:**
```
"I'm not going in there," Mei whispered, tugging at my sleeve.
"We have to," I replied. "There's no other way out."
```

- New speaker → **new paragraph**
- Comma inside the inverted commas before *said/whispered/replied*
- Add an **action beat** instead of always writing *he said*: *He looked away. "It wasn't me."*

**Cut dialogue that does nothing:**
❌ *"Hello." "Hi." "How are you?" "I am fine, thank you. And you?"*
This wastes 20 words and adds nothing.

## PART 9 — Phrase Banks

### Building tension
- My heart hammered against my ribs.
- Time seemed to slow to a crawl.
- A cold knot formed in the pit of my stomach.
- Every second stretched into an eternity.
- The silence was so complete that I could hear my own breathing.
- I did not dare to look.

### Describing a place
- The void deck was deserted, save for a stray cat curled in one corner.
- Sunlight filtered through the leaves, dappling the pavement with gold.
- The corridor smelt faintly of disinfectant and old paper.
- Rain drummed steadily on the zinc roof above us.

### Showing regret
- The words left my mouth before I could stop them.
- I would have given anything to take it back.
- Her expression was the kind I would remember for years.
- Something in me shrank when I saw her face.

### Showing determination
- I gritted my teeth and pressed on.
- Giving up now was not an option.
- I forced my trembling legs to move.
- One more step. Then another.

### Transitions (use sparingly, and vary them)
Moments later · Before long · By the time · No sooner had I… than · As the minutes crawled past · The next thing I knew · Little did I know · It was only later that I realised

## PART 10 — Model Composition 1 (Annotated)

**Theme: Honesty** · *Pictures: a wallet, a school gate, a police post*

---

> **The Wallet**
>
> The brown leather wallet was lying in the drain grating outside the school gate, half hidden by a soggy flyer. ⟵ *① Action/object opening. Specific detail: "soggy flyer".*
>
> I glanced around. The afternoon crowd had thinned, and nobody seemed to be looking for anything. I picked it up. Inside were three fifty-dollar notes, crisp and new, and a photograph of a man holding a toddler on his shoulders. ⟵ *② Problem established. The photograph is a deliberate choice — it makes the owner a person, not just a wallet.*
>
> One hundred and fifty dollars. I thought of the bicycle I had been saving for since March, the one I was still eighty dollars short of. My fingers tightened around the notes. Nobody had seen me. Nobody would ever know.
>
> But the toddler in the photograph was still grinning up at me. ⟵ *③ Climax begins — internal conflict, not external drama.*
>
> I stood there for what felt like an hour, the wallet growing heavier in my hand. A bus rumbled past. Somewhere behind me, a child laughed. My mother's voice surfaced in my head, unbidden: *"Rachel, the things you do when nobody is watching are the things that tell you who you are."* My throat tightened. Slowly, I closed the wallet.
>
> The police post was a ten-minute walk in the wrong direction. I walked it anyway. ⟵ *④ Decision shown through action. Two short sentences for impact.*
>
> Three days later, a man appeared at our door with a bag of oranges and the same toddler on his hip. He had wanted to thank me in person, he said. He was a delivery driver, and the money had been that week's earnings. ⟵ *⑤ Falling action — realistic reward, not a cash prize.*
>
> I never did get the bicycle that year. I saved for two more months and bought it in August instead. But every time I ride past that drain grating, I remember that I very nearly became someone else that afternoon — and that being honest cost me eight weeks and nothing more. ⟵ *⑥ Reflective ending. The lesson is shown, not announced.*

**Word count: ~330** · **Why it scores well:** one simple event, a fully developed internal climax, no melodrama, realistic outcome, varied sentence length, strong final line.

## PART 11 — Model Composition 2 (Annotated)

**Theme: A Difficult Decision** · *Pictures: a friend crying, a mobile phone, a teacher*

---

> **What I Owed Her**
>
> "You can't tell anyone. Promise me." ⟵ *Dialogue opening — instant tension.*
>
> Nadia's voice was barely a whisper, but it filled the empty stairwell between the third and fourth floors. Her eyes were red-rimmed, and she kept twisting the strap of her bag around her fingers.
>
> She had just told me that Suri and two other girls had been sending her messages for the past three weeks. Not the loud, obvious kind of bullying that teachers notice — the quiet kind, the kind that arrives at eleven at night and is deleted by morning. ⟵ *Problem established with a precise, believable detail.*
>
> "Promise," she repeated.
>
> I promised.
>
> For four days I kept that promise, and for four days I watched my best friend grow smaller. She stopped putting up her hand in class. She stopped eating during recess. On Thursday, she did not come to school at all. ⟵ *Rising action — the escalation is shown through small changes, not stated.*
>
> That evening I sat on my bed with my phone in my hand for a very long time. ⟵ *Climax begins.*
>
> Mrs Devi's number was saved in my contacts from our class group chat. All I had to do was press call. But Nadia had trusted me — had chosen me, out of everyone — and I could still hear her voice in that stairwell. If I told, she might never forgive me. If I stayed silent, I would keep watching her disappear.
>
> My thumb hovered over the screen. The room was so quiet I could hear the fan clicking. I thought about Thursday, about her empty chair. Then I pressed call. ⟵ *Slow-motion climax: sensory detail, thought, decision, short final sentence.*
>
> Nadia did not speak to me for two weeks. They were the longest two weeks of my life. ⟵ *Falling action — realistic consequence.*
>
> On the fifteenth day, she sat down beside me at recess and pushed half her sandwich towards me without a word. It was not an apology, and it was not forgiveness. It was something better than both. ⟵ *Understated, memorable ending.*
>
> I have learnt that keeping a promise is not always the same as keeping a friend safe. ⟵ *One clear line of reflection.*

**Word count: ~350**

## PART 12 — Ten Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Story drifts off-theme | Write the theme at the top of your plan and check it after every paragraph |
| Too many events | One problem, one climax. Cut everything else |
| The climax is two sentences long | Apply the slow-motion technique (Part 4) |
| Tense keeps switching | Choose simple past and stay there |
| Every sentence starts with "I" or "Then" | Use the sentence-opening table (Part 7) |
| Telling emotions: "I was very sad" | Use the Show-Don't-Tell bank in `04_Vocabulary_Bank.md` |
| Dialogue punctuated wrongly | Revise Section 12.1 of `01_Grammar_Reference.md` |
| Fantasy or violent plots | Examiners reward believability. Keep it grounded |
| Moral bolted on at the end | The lesson must grow out of the story |
| No time left to check | Reserve the last 5 minutes for editing |

## PART 13 — Time Management (50 minutes)

| Minutes | Task |
|---|---|
| 0–6 | Read the theme and pictures. Complete the 6-Box Plan. |
| 6–10 | Write the opening paragraph carefully — it sets your language level. |
| 10–40 | Write paragraphs 2–5, giving the climax the most space. |
| 40–45 | Write the ending. |
| 45–50 | **Edit** using the checklist below. |

---

## PART 14 — Five-Minute Self-Editing Checklist

Read your composition **backwards, sentence by sentence** — it forces you to see the words rather than the story.

- [ ] Does every sentence have a **subject and a verb**?
- [ ] Is the **tense** consistent throughout?
- [ ] **Subject–verb agreement**: check every sentence with a plural subject
- [ ] Are all **plurals** correct? (no missing -s)
- [ ] Are **capital letters** used for every sentence start and proper noun?
- [ ] Is every piece of **dialogue** punctuated correctly and on a new paragraph?
- [ ] Any **spelling** you are unsure of? Swap in a word you can spell.
- [ ] Did you **repeat** any word too often? (especially *said, went, nice, very, then*)
- [ ] Is the **theme** unmistakable to someone who has not read the question?
- [ ] Is your **final sentence** strong?
