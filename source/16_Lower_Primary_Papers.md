# Primary 3 Practice Paper

A Primary 3 school-examination paper. This is not a PSLE paper: it is shorter, there is no synthesis section, and the comprehension is mostly about finding information in the passage.

**60 marks · 75 minutes.** Sit it in one go, with a timer, and do not look anything up. Mark it afterwards against the answers at the end.

## Booklet B · Comprehension  ·  18 marks

*Read the passage and answer the questions. Note the marks for each question.*

The Mango Tree

There is a mango tree behind our block. It is older than my father.

Every year, in April, it makes far more mangoes than one family could eat. Most of them fall onto the grass and spoil, because nobody can reach the high branches.

Last April, my grandfather had an idea. He tied an old plastic bottle to the end of a long bamboo pole. He cut the bottom off the bottle first, so that a mango would drop straight into it.

It worked. In one afternoon we picked forty-one mangoes.

That was far too many for us. My grandfather washed them, put them into paper bags, and wrote a number on each bag. Then he gave one bag to every flat on our floor. There are eight flats, so each bag had five mangoes in it, and one was left over for him.

The next morning, somebody had left a tin of biscuits outside our door. There was no note.

A week later, Mrs Rahim from the flat at the end knocked and gave us a jar of mango chutney she had made from her bag. She said she had not spoken to anybody on our floor for two years.

The tree is still there. My grandfather still has the bamboo pole behind the door, and he keeps the bottle on it all year, even in the months when there is nothing at all to pick.

1. Where is the mango tree? *(1m)*

2. Why did most of the mangoes spoil before the grandfather's idea? *(2m)*

3. Describe the tool the grandfather made. *(2m)*

4. How many mangoes did they pick in one afternoon? *(1m)*

5. How do we know the grandfather wanted to be fair to the neighbours? *(2m)*

6. Who do you think left the tin of biscuits, and why is the writer not certain? *(2m)*

7. What did Mrs Rahim do with her mangoes, and what did she tell the family when she came? *(2m)*

8. How do we know the neighbours were grateful? Give one piece of evidence. *(1m)*

9. Which word in the passage means 'go bad'? *(1m)*

10. What does the grandfather hope will happen next April, and how do we know? *(2m)*

11. Why do you think the grandfather wrote a number on each paper bag? *(2m)*

### Booklet B · Comprehension

- **1** It is behind their block of flats.
- **2** They fell onto the grass and were wasted, because nobody could reach the high branches to pick them.
- **3** He tied an old plastic bottle to the end of a long bamboo pole, and cut the bottom off the bottle so that a mango would drop straight into it.
- **4** Forty-one.
- **5** He gave a bag to every flat on the floor, put the same number of mangoes in each bag, and kept only the one that was left over for himself.
- **6** It was probably one of the neighbours who had been given a bag of mangoes. The writer cannot be certain because there was no note with the tin.
- **7** She made mango chutney from them and brought a jar of it to the family. She told them she had not spoken to anybody on their floor for two years.
- **8** Either: somebody left a tin of biscuits outside their door the next morning; or Mrs Rahim brought them a jar of mango chutney a week later.
- **9** 'Spoil'.
- **10** He hopes there will be another crop of mangoes to pick and share. We know because he keeps the bottle on the bamboo pole all year round, ready, even in the months when there is nothing to pick.
- **11** So that he could make sure every bag held the same number of mangoes and every flat got a fair share. It shows he had counted them and wanted to divide them evenly.

The Quiet Shop

There is a bookshop near my aunt's flat that has a sign on the door reading NO TALKING, PLEASE. It is not a library. It is a shop, and things in it are for sale.

The owner, Mr Pillai, is not unfriendly. He nods when you come in and nods when you leave. If you need to ask him something, you write it on one of the small yellow squares of paper beside the till, and he writes back underneath.

My aunt says he has run the shop that way for eleven years, and that when he first put the sign up, customers stopped coming. For six months the shop was nearly empty.

Then something changed. People who wanted to be left alone began to travel there on purpose — from other estates, and then from other parts of the island. A newspaper wrote about it. Now the shop is busier on a Saturday than any bookshop in the mall, and it is completely silent.

The first time my aunt took me, I found it uncomfortable. I kept wanting to say something about a book I had found, and there was nobody to say it to. I bought nothing and was glad to leave.

The second time, I stayed for two hours.

What changed was a girl of about six at the shelf by the window. She had found a book about sharks and was holding it open with both arms because it was too big for her hands. She did not say anything. She simply turned it round and showed it to me, and I nodded, and she went back to reading. The whole exchange took four seconds and I have remembered it for three years.

I do not think the sign is really about noise. I think Mr Pillai worked out that most of us have nowhere at all to go where nothing is expected of us, and that this is worth more than conversation.

I have written him two notes in three years. He has answered both. I still do not know what his voice sounds like.
